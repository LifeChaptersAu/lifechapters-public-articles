# What Equipment Do I Need To Record My Nan?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/bb2b16b28499a07b182c.delivery-v1.webp?hero=3052d4639d9bc1dc>)

You do not need a studio. You need three things: a smartphone (or a basic digital voice recorder under $100), a quiet room with soft furnishings to absorb echo, and a simple list of prompts. The quality of the connection matters more than the bitrate of the file.

Standing in a music store, holding a microphone box that feels heavier than expected, or scrolling through online reviews where the price tag makes you hesitate and put the item back, can make the task feel impossible. The technical language is dense, and the advice often assumes you are a professional archivist. For most of us, standing in the kitchen with a phone in one hand and a cup of tea in the other, the question is simpler: is this enough?

The fear of hearing your own voice in the playback is often the biggest barrier. We worry that the recording will sound "bad," or that we are doing it wrong, so we wait for the ideal setup that never arrives. You do not need to buy expensive hardware to capture a life story.

The person who wants to preserve their nan's voice but feels paralyzed by the choice of equipment is not an audio engineer. They are the grandchild, the daughter, or the son who just wants to hear her story clearly, without the stress of a production crew.

## Do I need to buy a professional microphone to record my nan?

No. For a family conversation, a professional microphone is rarely necessary. A standard smartphone microphone, held within a foot or two of the speaker, captures the human voice with enough fidelity for family members to listen to years later. The goal is clarity, not archival perfection.

If you prefer a dedicated device, a basic digital voice recorder is a good option. Brands like Zoom or Tascam make models that are small, simple, and available at Australian music stores or online retailers like JB Hi-Fi. These devices are designed for spoken word, not music production, so they handle the nuances of speech well without requiring you to understand signal flow or audio interfaces.

The key is that the device is simple to operate. If you have to fiddle with settings while your nan is talking, you are losing the moment. A phone or a simple button-press recorder keeps your attention on the conversation, not the technology.

## Is my smartphone good enough to capture her voice clearly?

Yes, provided you manage the environment. Modern smartphones have excellent microphones, and for a one-on-one conversation, they are more than sufficient. The quality of the recording depends less on the hardware and more on the distance from the speaker and the amount of background noise.

If you are using a phone, prop it up on a stable surface, such as a stack of magazines or a book, so it is at a consistent height. Do not hold it in your hand for long periods, as this introduces handling noise and makes it difficult to relax. Place the phone about thirty centimetres to one metre away from your nan. This distance captures the voice clearly without picking up every breath or lip movement, which can feel intrusive.

You do not need to worry about file formats like 24-bit or 96kHz. These are standards for music production and are overkill for family listening. The standard audio format on your phone is perfectly fine for sharing with family. The goal is accessibility, so that anyone in the family can open the file and hear the story without needing special software.

## How do I set up the room so the recording doesn't sound echoey?

Echo is a common technical issue. In a tiled kitchen, clapping your hands can sound like a cave, with the sound bouncing off hard surfaces and creating a hollow, reverb-heavy effect. To reduce this, choose a room with soft furnishings. A bedroom with a bed and curtains, or a lounge room with a sofa and rugs, is ideal.

Avoid empty rooms with tiled floors and bare walls. If you must record in such a space, you can drape a blanket over a chair or use a closet filled with clothes as a makeshift acoustic booth. The soft materials absorb the sound waves, preventing them from bouncing back.

Turn off the TV, the radio, and any air conditioning units that create a constant hum. These background noises are difficult to remove in post-production and can distract listeners. A quiet room with soft furnishings is all you need to get a clear, natural-sounding recording.

## What is the difference between recording audio and video for a life story?

Audio is generally less intimidating for the person being recorded. When you turn on a camera, it can feel like a performance, and some people find it harder to relax.

Audio allows for a more relaxed, conversational dynamic. You can sit next to each other, look at old photos, and let the conversation drift naturally. If you do choose to record video, keep it simple. A static shot of the two of you talking is enough. You do not need multiple angles, lighting rigs, or a tripod. The focus should be on the connection, not the production value.

Many families find that they prefer audio because it is easier to share. A video file is large and can be cumbersome to send, whereas an audio file is small and easy to email or upload to a cloud service. If your nan is comfortable with video, it is a wonderful addition, but it is not a requirement.

## How do I handle the fear that the recording will be "bad"?

A "bad" recording with a clear, emotional story is infinitely more valuable than a "perfect" recording of silence or a rehearsed script. The value is in the words, the tone, and the memory, not in the technical quality of the file.

If you are worried about the process feeling like an interview, which can make the conversation feel stiff, you might find it helpful to read [how to record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>). The goal is to create a safe space where your nan feels comfortable sharing, not to produce a broadcast-quality podcast.

Remember that you are not an archivist. You are a family member whose job is to listen, to ask gentle questions, and to let the story unfold. The technology is just a tool to help you do that. If the recording is a bit noisy, or if there is a pause, it does not matter. What matters is that the story is captured.

## What people get wrong

**Buying the most expensive gear.** This delays the process and adds unnecessary stress, so a simple phone or a basic recorder is enough. Spend your energy on preparing questions, not on selecting microphones.

**Waiting for the perfect conditions.** The perfect room, the perfect time, the perfect mood. These rarely align; start with what you have. A rough recording made this month is better than a perfect one that never happens.

**Focusing on the technical side.** If you are constantly adjusting settings, checking levels, or worrying about the file format, you are not present. Put the phone down, or set it and forget it, and focus on the conversation.

**Forcing a long session.** If your nan is tired or distracted, stop; a ten-minute recording is a success; do not push for an hour. Comfort is the priority, especially if your nan has dementia or memory concerns. [Dementia Australia](<https://www.dementiaguide.com.au/>) suggests keeping sessions short and respectful of the person's energy levels.

If your nan becomes emotional during the conversation, which is a common and healthy part of the process, you can read [what to do if my grandparent gets emotional during a life story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-do-if-my-grandparent-gets-emotional-during-a-life-story>) for guidance on how to support them.

Start with one conversation; Pick a quiet time this week, sit with your nan, and ask one simple question: "What was your favourite thing to eat when you were a child?" Record it on your phone. You don't need to buy anything else to start.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What equipment do I need to record my nan?

You need a smartphone or a basic digital voice recorder, a quiet room with soft furnishings to absorb echo, and a simple list of prompts. The quality of the connection matters more than the bitrate of the file, so you do not need a studio or expensive hardware.

### Can I just use my laptop's built-in microphone?

Yes, it is usually sufficient for a one-on-one conversation in a quiet room. A smartphone held close often captures slightly clearer high frequencies, but a laptop microphone is perfectly fine for capturing the voice and the story.

### Do I need to edit the recording before sharing it?

No. Raw, unedited recordings are often more authentic and valuable for family members. Editing is optional, not required. The natural pauses and tone are part of the human connection.

### What if my nan speaks quietly or has a voice tremor?

Sit closer, within thirty centimetres, and speak softly yourself to encourage her to match your volume. Use the phone's built-in noise reduction if available, but the most important thing is to be patient and attentive.

### Is it better to record video or audio?

Audio is less intimidating for the grandparent and easier to manage. Video is optional and should only be done if she is comfortable being seen. The focus should be on the conversation, not the visual production.

### How long should the recording be?

As long as she is comfortable; ten minutes is a great start; do not aim for an hour. Short, frequent sessions are often more effective and less tiring for both parties.
