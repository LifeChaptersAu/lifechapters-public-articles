# What If They Say I Don't Want To Talk About This?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/bb2b16b28499a07b182c.delivery-v1.webp?hero=762a838abe4f4b00>)

Validate the boundary straight away and don't take it personally. Say their comfort matters more to you than getting the whole story, then move gently to a different chapter of their life. A refusal is them exercising choice over their own story, and the finished book is truer for it.

This is for the person who asked their dad about something — the years in uniform, the job he left, the brother he stopped speaking to — and got a flat *I don't want to talk about this*. It wasn't angry or upset, just a door closing politely. It stings more than it should because you weren't prying, you were interested, and now you're sitting there feeling like you did something wrong. If you've gone quiet since, or stopped asking anything at all in case you hit another wall, you're not the only one.

Here's the thing worth knowing before anything else: that refusal is not a failure of the conversation. It's a person deciding what belongs to them. The way you respond in the next ten seconds decides whether they keep talking to you about anything else at all.

## Why does my parent's refusal to talk feel like a personal rejection?

Because you offered something and it was declined, and the two things sit very close together in the chest. You asked out of love, so a no lands as though the love was the thing rejected. It almost never is. When someone shuts down a subject, they're usually protecting themselves, or protecting you, or protecting someone else who isn't in the room. A subject can be too raw, too shameful in their own eyes, or simply somewhere they've decided not to go again, and none of that is a verdict on you.

There's also a quieter version of this in a lot of Australian families, where the instinct is to avoid drama or being any trouble. That can come out as silence rather than a straight conversation about why the answer is no. You get a shrug, a change of subject, a *nothing to tell there* — and the reason stays private.

The reframe that helps most: you are not owed the story. You're being let in, one room at a time, and one closed door doesn't mean the house is shut.

## How can I validate their boundary without making them feel guilty?

Answer fast, answer warm, and don't ask why. The speed is the whole thing, because a pause before you accept it reads as disappointment, and they'll feel it. Something as simple as *that's completely fine, I'd rather you tell me what you want to tell me* does the job. What you're saying underneath is: your comfort matters more to me than the completeness of this.

Avoid the two moves that create guilt. The first is negotiating — *are you sure? Not even a bit of it?* — which tells them the no wasn't accepted. The second is going flat and hurt, which puts them in the position of having to manage your feelings on top of their own. Most parents will then either force out a thin version of the story to make you feel better, or quietly decide these conversations aren't worth having.

And then keep going. The strongest signal that a boundary is genuinely fine is that the conversation carries on comfortably afterwards.

## What are three specific phrases to pivot the conversation smoothly?

A pivot is a bridge: you accept the closed door and open a nearby one in the same breath, so there's no dead air for either of you to sit in. These three cover most situations.

**"I completely respect that; What was daily life like for you around then?"** — same era, no event. You're asking about routines, not the thing itself, and people will often talk freely about the surrounding life.

**"Fair enough; Tell me about a time you did enjoy, then — what period would you go back to?"** — hands them the steering wheel. Letting them choose the next subject restores the sense that this is their story.

**"That's alright. Can I ask about someone instead? What was your boss like?"** — a person rather than a period. Other people are easier to describe than your own hard years, and the story of the person usually carries the era along with it.

Say it in your own words rather than reciting these. If the answers that follow stay short and thin, that's a separate problem with its own fixes — [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) covers it.

## Why is respecting silence essential for a good legacy book?

Because what you're making is a portrait of a person, not a biography written by their child. Those are different things. A portrait is true when it shows who someone is, including what they chose to keep. If a whole chapter is missing because they didn't want it in there, the book is still true. Arguably it's truer, because their judgement shaped it. A book assembled over someone's objections carries something you can feel on the page — a slight flinch in it — and the people reading it in thirty years will be reading what a person was willing to say, which is the honest record.

There's a practical side too. People who feel in control of what goes in tend to volunteer far more than people who feel interrogated. The surest way to get less is to push for more.

## A Saturday afternoon in Toowoomba

Rachel asked her dad, Bill, about his army years. He'd been asked before and had always deflected, and this time he put his cup down and said, plainly, *I don't want to talk about this*. She felt the heat come up her neck. Twenty minutes of good conversation and she'd walked straight into it.

What she said was: "No worries, Dad. What was the house like when you got back — where were you living?" He told her about a boarding house in Toowoomba with a landlady who rationed the hot water, and the job he took at the timber yard because it started at six and he was awake anyway. Then, half an hour later and without being asked, one sentence about a bloke he'd served with. Then back to the timber yard.

She never got the army years. She got the man who came home from them, which is the part that explains everything else. If she had pressed at the cup-down moment, she'd have had neither.

## How do I handle the guilt of not getting the "full story"?

Notice that the guilt is built on an idea that doesn't hold up — that there's a complete version of a life somewhere, and you've failed to collect it. There isn't. Every account of a person is partial, including the one they'd give about themselves on their best day. What you can hold onto instead: the goal is connection, not data collection. An afternoon where your father felt respected and talked for an hour about a boarding house is not a failed session.

It also helps to be honest about whose discomfort you're solving. Pressing for the hard chapter is often about the child's need to understand, not the parent's need to tell. Both are real, but only one of them gets to set the pace, and it's theirs. If the refusal came with tears or a subject that clearly hurt, that's worth reading about separately — [what to do if they get emotional during a life story conversation](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-do-if-my-grandparent-gets-emotional-during-a-life-story>) deals with the moment itself.

## What if they refuse to talk about a major life event entirely?

Let it go, and build around it. A war, a marriage that ended, a child who died, a stretch of years they've sealed — these get refused most often, and they're exactly the ones where pressing does the most damage. Ask instead about the shape it left. Not the event, but the life around it: what they did with their days in that period, who they leaned on, what they believed about work or family afterwards. You'll often find you understand the missing chapter better through its outline than you would have through a reluctant account of it.

Sometimes the door opens later on its own, and sometimes it doesn't. Both are alright. If a story never gets told, that was theirs to decide. One caution. If someone's refusal comes with confusion or real distress rather than a clear no, particularly where there's dementia in the picture, treat comfort as the only priority and stop. Dementia Australia's guidance on communication and person-centred care is the right place to read about it, and a GP or a specialist nurse is the right person to ask about consent and capacity — not an article.

## What people get wrong

Treating a no as a negotiating position is a common mistake, but it isn't an opening bid. Asking twice teaches them the first answer doesn't count, and the cost is every conversation after this one.

Making the silence mean something is another trap. People decide the refusal is proof of a secret, then go looking. Often there's nothing there but tiredness, or a story they've told enough times and are done with.

Going quiet afterwards is the most common error. The subject is dropped and so is everything else. Keep talking; the recovery matters more than the refusal.

Deciding they'll never record anything is a final error. One closed subject isn't a closed book, though a blanket refusal is a different problem — [what to do if they refuse to record their life story at all](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-do-i-do-if-my-grandparent-refuses-to-record-their-life-story>) covers that one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it rude to keep asking after my parent says no?

Yes, asking again after a clear no tends to cost you more than the story is worth. Accepting it the first time is what makes the next conversation possible, because they learn that talking to you is safe. If the subject matters to them too, it usually comes back around on their terms.

### How do I know if they are refusing because they are sad or just tired?

Often you won't know, and it doesn't change what you do. Validate the boundary and move to something lighter either way. If it was tiredness, they'll warm up again in a few minutes or on another day; if it was sadness, you've given them room without making them explain themselves, which is the kindest response to both.

### Can I ask a sibling to try again later?

Yes, if that sibling has a genuinely different relationship with them, since parents open up differently to different children. What it shouldn't be is a second attempt at the same pressure from a new angle. If they say no again, that's the answer, and it isn't a job to hand around the family.

### What if I feel like I'm failing as a child by not getting the story?

You're not failing, you're respecting someone's wishes, which is the harder thing to do. The point of these conversations is connection rather than collection. An hour where your parent felt comfortable and talked about ordinary things is worth more than a complete record extracted from someone who didn't want to give it.
