# How Do I Ask My Nan About Her School Days Without Her Shutting Down?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/b0110eab333e684c23aa.delivery-v1.webp?hero=50e6848335a36b88>)

Stop asking "How was school?" and start asking about a specific sensory detail, like "What did your lunchbox smell like?" or "Who was the teacher with the strictest rules?" Specific, concrete questions bypass the need to summarize a whole day and invite a single, manageable memory, which lowers the pressure on Nan and opens the door for her to share naturally.

You are sitting in the worn armchair by the window, the smell of burnt toast still hanging in the air, and you want to know about her childhood. You ask, "How was school?" and she gives you a shrug, or a flat "It was fine," and the moment evaporates. It can feel like a rejection of you, or perhaps of the idea that her life's interesting enough to keep.

You are not being nosy, you are trying to connect. But the question is too big, and it asks her to perform a summary of years she lived through decades ago. That is a heavy lift, and most people, especially elders, will decline it to protect their energy.

The fix is simple. You do not need a script or a professional interview technique. You just need to change the shape of the question from a broad report to a small, specific memory.

## Why does "How was school?" shut down the conversation?

When you ask "How was school?", you are asking for an evaluation. It requires her to look back, gather facts, weigh them, and form a judgment. That is cognitive work. If she is tired, or if the memory is fuzzy, the easiest answer is "It was fine."

This cognitive load is significant for elders. They need a low-pressure entry point to access these memories without the strain of reconstructing a timeline.

If you are dealing with short answers in general, it helps to understand that brevity is often a boundary, not a lack of interest. You can read more about [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) to see how to pivot without pushing.

## How do I ask my nan about her school days without sounding like a journalist?

The key is specificity. Instead of asking about the whole institution, ask about one object, one sound, or one person.

These questions are easy to answer because they are sensory. They do not require a narrative structure; they just require a picture. Once she starts describing the chalk dust or the lunchbox, the story often unfolds on its own.

This approach respects her autonomy. She can answer with a single detail if she wants, or she can expand if she feels like it. You are not demanding a report; you are inviting a glimpse. ## What do I do if Nan gives a one-word answer?

If she says "It was fine" or "I don't remember," do not push. That is a stop signal. It means she is not ready, or she does not have the energy to reconstruct that memory right now.

Acknowledge it gently; "That sounds like it was a lot," or "I imagine it was a busy time." Then change the subject. Go back to the tea, or look at a photo together.

If she shuts down, it is not a failure. It is data. It tells you that this particular question, at this particular time, is not the right one. Try a different angle later, or let it go.

## How do I handle it when the topic feels too heavy?

School days can be light, but they can also be heavy. Bullying, strict discipline, or family issues from that era can surface. If the conversation turns somber, let her lead.

Do not try to fix it or offer advice. Just listen. If she becomes distressed, gently redirect to a current, pleasant topic. "Let's look at this photo of the garden," or "Tell me about the tea you are making."

If you are worried about her mental health or memory, it is worth checking in with a professional. For general guidance on communication with dementia, [Dementia Australia](<https://dementia.org.au/>) offers resources on how to support meaningful conversations.

## Why do side-by-side conversations work better than face-to-face?

Face-to-face can feel like an interrogation because you are looking at her, waiting for an answer, which creates pressure.

Side-by-side reduces that pressure; sort through old photos together, walk in the garden, or bake a cake. The task gives you both something to do with your hands, and it takes the spotlight off the conversation.

## How do I keep the connection going after the first chat?

One conversation is not enough to capture a life. And that is okay. You do not need to finish the story in one sitting.

The best way to keep the connection going is to follow up. If she mentioned a specific teacher or friend, ask about them next time. "How was that teacher you mentioned last week?" This shows you were listening, and it makes the next conversation easier to start.

You can also share small details of your own day. "I had a hard day at work, but your story about the school bus made me smile." This balances the exchange and shows that you are a participant, not just an interviewer.

## What people get wrong

**Asking for a summary.** "Tell me about your school days" is too big, so break it down into small, sensory pieces.

**Pushing when she says no.** "I don't remember" is a boundary, so respect it and try a different topic or a different day.

**Making it a performance.** Face-to-face, with a recorder, can feel like a test, whereas side-by-side, with a task, feels like a chat.

**Expecting perfection.** The first conversation will be rough, but that is fine because the goal is connection, not a polished transcript.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What if my nan gets emotional when talking about school?

It is okay to pause. Acknowledge the feeling by saying, "That sounds like it was a lot," and let her lead the next step. If she becomes distressed, gently redirect to a current, pleasant topic like looking at photos or enjoying a snack.

### How do I ask my nan about her school days if she has dementia?

Focus on the present moment and sensory details; avoid complex timelines or dates. If she becomes distressed, gently redirect to a current, pleasant topic. For specific communication strategies, consult resources from Dementia Australia or a qualified healthcare professional.

### What are some good questions to ask a grandparent about their childhood?

Ask about specific objects like toys or clothes, people like teachers or friends, or places like the schoolyard or the canteen. Questions like "What did the school bell sound like?" or "Who was your best friend?" are easy to answer and invite detailed memories.

### Why does my nan shut down when I ask about the past?

She may feel she is being judged, or she may simply not have the energy to reconstruct a narrative. Specific, low-pressure questions help because they bypass the need for a summary and invite a single, manageable memory instead.

### How long should I wait before asking about school again?

There is no fixed rule, but waiting a few days or weeks allows the initial pressure to fade. When you return to the topic, try a different sensory angle, such as asking about the weather that day or a specific game, to show you are curious about her experience rather than demanding a full history.
