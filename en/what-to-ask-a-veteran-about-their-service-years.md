# What To Ask A Veteran About Their Service Years?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/53aec536e8f5aefd21f4.delivery-v1.webp?hero=16211089324e7089>)

Ask about the sensory details of a specific day, such as the smell of the mess tin or the sound of the radio. Move from the official timeline to the human experience by asking what they were thinking about, not just what they were doing. This invites the raw, unfiltered memory that defines their character.

You have sat down with your father, and the air smells faintly of the diesel and wet wool that still clings to his old uniform. You asked the obvious question about his time in the Army. He gave you the date he enlisted, the branch he served in, and then the conversation stopped. It feels like you have hit a brick wall, and you are left wondering if you are asking the wrong things, or if he simply does not want to share.

The "official" story is often a rehearsed summary, a safe version of events that has been told many times. When asked broadly, he might say, "It was hard work, but I did my duty." That is a flat, rehearsed answer. The real story, the one with the fear, the boredom, the camaraderie, and the small human moments, is usually buried under that surface. A vivid answer sounds different: "The mess tin always smelled of burnt tinned beef, and the radio in the corner played the same three songs on repeat until we stopped hearing them." You are not failing because you are not a historian. You are failing because you are asking for a report when you are hoping for a memory.

The goal is not to document a military history. It is to capture a person.

## Why does "How was your service?" shut down the conversation?

When you ask this, you watch him check his watch, or perhaps he looks away toward the window, his gaze fixed on something distant. That physical reaction signals that the question has failed. It asks someone to summarise years of their life in a single sentence. It invites a generalisation, not a memory. When a veteran hears "how was it," they often reach for the most neutral, safe answer they have, which is usually a flat description of duty. It does not give them a place to stand in the past.

If you find yourself getting short, one-word answers, it is not a lack of interest. It is a lack of a hook. You might find it helpful to look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) to understand why these minimal responses happen and how to gently steer back to the details.

## What are the 5 sensory questions that open deeper memories?

Start with the physical world they inhabited. Ask: "What did the barracks or the ship smell like?" This specificity demonstrates the principle that memory is sensory, not chronological. Once you ask about the smell, the mind goes back to a particular moment.

Other questions that tend to open the door include:

- What was the first song you heard on your first deployment?
- What did the uniform feel like on your skin after a long day?
- What was the sound of the radio in the mess?
- What did you eat for breakfast on a typical day?

These questions are safe and specific, and they force the mind to go back to a particular moment, and once they are there, the emotions and the context usually follow naturally. You are not asking for a report; you are asking for a scene.

## How do you ask about difficult times without triggering trauma?

If the conversation turns to a heavy topic, such as a specific incident, and you sense the weight of it, pivot to a lighter sensory detail. For example, if he mentions a difficult night, you might ask, "What did the sky look like that night?" or "What was the first thing you did when the sun came up?" This respectful pivot moves the focus from the emotional intensity of the event to the physical reality of the environment.

The key is to ask about their thoughts, not their feelings. Instead of "How did you feel when you were under fire?" try "What were you thinking about in that moment?" This shifts the focus from an emotional reaction to a cognitive process. It is less intrusive and often more revealing. If they become distressed, or if you sense they are closing down, you have the right to stop. Comfort and permission always take priority over completing the recording. If they refuse to talk about a specific period, respect that boundary. You can find more guidance on [how to handle your parent's silence when they refuse to talk about their](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-handle-my-parents-silence-when-they-refuse-to-talk-about-their>) past in this linked article.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it like an interview.** If you sit with a laptop open and a notepad in hand, you are conducting an interrogation. The goal is a conversation. Let the questions be a guide, not a script. If they mention a specific person or place, follow that thread, even if it is not on your list.

**Correcting the facts.** If they get a date wrong, or if a detail seems off, let it go. You are not writing a history book; you are capturing how they remember it; that memory is the truth for them. Arguing over facts kills the flow and makes them defensive.

**Pushing for the "big" moments.** The most powerful stories are often about the small things. The way the light came through the window, the taste of the food, the sound of a specific engine. These details are the anchors that hold the memory together. Do not skip them to get to the "important" events.

**Assuming they want to talk about the war.** For many veterans, the service was just a part of their life. They might prefer to talk about their friends, their hobbies, or the people they met. Let them choose the focus; your role is to listen, not to direct.

## How do you handle the silence after a powerful answer?

Sometimes, after a deep answer, there is a pause. It can feel awkward. You want to fill the silence, but you should not. Let the silence sit. It is often in the silence that the next, deeper thought is forming.

If the silence stretches too long and you need to move on, do not jump to a new topic. Instead, ask a gentle follow-up. For example, if he describes a specific location, ask, "Who was sitting next to you when you saw that?" This specific bridge keeps the conversation flowing without breaking the emotional connection. If you are struggling to maintain this flow, you might find it helpful to look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) for more practical tips on maintaining momentum.

## What is the difference between an interview and a conversation?

When a veteran senses they are being interviewed, they often stiffen, their posture becoming rigid as they prepare a formal, expected response. They are performing a role. When they feel they are sharing, their shoulders drop, they lean in, and their voice softens. They are no longer performing; they are connecting.

This distinction matters because veterans are often used to being interviewed. By media, by officials, by researchers; they have a script for that. By making it a conversation, you bypass the script. You become a person sitting across the table, and they become a person sharing a memory. The result is different. It is less polished, but it is more real.

## How do you turn these fragmented answers into a coherent story?

You will have recordings, notes, and memories; they will be scattered; some will be detailed, some will be vague; that is normal. The goal is not to have a perfect transcript. The goal is to have the raw material.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What to ask a veteran about their service years?

Ask about the sensory details of a specific day, such as the smell of the mess tin or the sound of the radio. Move from the official timeline to the human experience by asking what they were thinking about, not just what they were doing. This invites the raw, unfiltered memory that defines their character.

### What if my dad gets angry when I ask about his service?

This is a common boundary; respect it immediately; do not push. You can try a different, lighter question later, or simply acknowledge his right to privacy. If he becomes upset, it is best to pause the conversation and return to it another day, or not at all.

### Do I need to be a historian to ask these questions?

No; you only need to be curious and respectful. The questions are designed to be accessible to anyone, regardless of military knowledge. You do not need to know the difference between a rifle and a shotgun, or the history of the ADF. You just need to be willing to listen.

### Is it too late to ask if my grandparent is in their 90s?

It is never too late to ask, but it is important to prioritize their comfort. If they are fatigued, keep sessions short (15-20 minutes). You can always come back later. The goal is to capture what they are willing to share, not to force a complete history.

### How do I ask about their service years if they seem stoic?

Yes, but frame it gently. Instead of "How did you feel?", try "What was going through your mind?" or "What were you thinking about in that moment?" This is less direct and often more inviting. It allows them to share their inner experience without feeling exposed.
