# What If My Mum Gets Upset That I’m Recording Her?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/e429783103cc23d6e560.delivery-v1.webp?hero=a02ddeeefde8139e>)

Her discomfort is a protective instinct, not a rejection of you. Pause the recording, validate her fear of being "pinned down," and explain that this is about preserving her voice for her own future, not for public consumption. Give her full control over what is kept, ensuring the process feels like a shared conversation rather than an interrogation.

You have the phone out, the app is open, and the red dot is blinking. Your mum looks at it, then at you, and suddenly the conversation has stopped. She pulls her hands into her lap, her posture tightens, and she says, *why are you doing this?* It feels like a rejection. You thought you were doing something kind, something for the family, and now you feel like you’ve intruded on her privacy.

This is not a failure of technology, and it is not a failure of your relationship. It is a very common, very human reaction to being recorded. For many people, especially those who grew up in eras where privacy was guarded tightly, the idea of their voice being captured and stored feels unsafe. It feels like being exposed.

If you have been sitting with this worry, wondering *What if my mum gets upset that I’m recording her*, you are not alone. The good news is that this reaction is manageable. It does not mean you have to stop trying to preserve her story. It means you need to change how you approach the recording, shifting from a mode of extraction to a mode of collaboration.

## Why does my mum feel unsafe when I turn on the recorder?

For many Australian parents, sharing personal history was often seen as "airing dirty laundry" or being vulnerable to judgment. There is also a specific anxiety about having one's voice used without context. She may be worried that her voice is being captured for something she doesn't understand or control.

The act of recording can feel like a clinical extraction. It turns a fluid, shared memory into a fixed, permanent object. If she feels that object is being used to judge her, or that it will be shared with people she isn't comfortable with, her instinct is to protect herself. This is not about you. It is about her sense of safety and agency.

## How do I stop the recording without ending the conversation?

You need a "Pause" Protocol. This is a concrete script you can use when you notice the tension rising. Say, "I hear that this feels too heavy; i’m turning the recorder off. We can just talk, or we can stop for today. There is no pressure to finish."

This does three things: it validates her fear, it gives her immediate control, and it separates the act of talking from the act of recording. She can still share the story with you, but without the pressure of it being captured. This often lowers the defensive wall. If she continues talking after the recorder is off, you can gently ask if she would be open to turning it back on for a minute, just to get the next bit. If not, let it stay off.

## What is the difference between "recording" and "capturing" a story?

"Recording" implies a one-way transfer of data; "capturing" implies a shared moment. When you frame it as capturing a story, you are inviting her into the process rather than subjecting her to it. It is the difference between being interviewed and being heard.

Try to shift the language in your head and in your words to her. Instead of "I want to record your life story," try "I want to capture a few of your favourite stories." This small change can make a big difference in how she perceives the activity. It is less about creating an archive and more about preserving a connection.

## How can I give my mum control over what gets kept?

Establish a "red button" agreement. Tell her that she can say "stop" at any time, and the recording will be paused or deleted immediately. This is not a suggestion; it is a promise. When she sees that you honour this boundary, her sense of safety increases.

You can also review the recordings together. If there is a part she is uncomfortable with, delete it in front of her. This act of deletion is powerful. It shows that her comfort matters more than the completeness of the archive. It reinforces that she is in charge of her own narrative.

## What should I say if she asks who else will hear this?

Be honest and specific. If it is just for the immediate family, say so. "It is just for us, the kids, and maybe your siblings if you want them to have it." If it is for a wider audience, explain why, but be prepared for her to say no. You do not need to justify it; you just need to respect the answer.

If she is worried about strangers hearing it, reassure her that the file is private. It is not going on the internet; it is not being sold; it is a family keepsake. This helps to dispel the fear of commercial exploitation or public judgment.

## How do I handle the silence after she says "I don't want to talk about that"?

Respect the silence; do not fill it with questions or explanations; let the silence sit. She may be processing, or she may be deciding what she is comfortable sharing. If she moves on to a different topic, follow her lead. If she stays silent, you can gently say, "Okay. Let's talk about something else."

This is where the "interview" feeling often creeps in. If you push, you are interviewing; if you wait, you are listening; listening is the foundation of trust. ## What people get wrong

**Insisting on the recording.** If she is uncomfortable, the recording is not the point. The relationship is; pushing for the recording damages the relationship.

**Assuming her discomfort is about you.** It is rarely about you; it is about the act of being recorded; do not take it personally.

**Ignoring the "exposure" fear.** Dismissing her concern as overthinking invalidates her feelings; acknowledge the fear; validate it; then offer a way to manage it.

**Forgetting the "gift to self" reframe.** The goal is not to create a document for others. It is to create a keepsake for her. When she is tired, or alone, or just wants to hear her own voice, she can listen to it. This is a gift to her, not just to the family.

## A kitchen in Perth, an afternoon in autumn

I remember sitting with my Nan in her kitchen. The light was low, and the tea was getting cold. I had my phone out, and she kept glancing at it. She didn't say anything, but the conversation was stiff. I realised I was treating her like a subject, not a person.

I put the phone face down. I picked up a photo of her young self, standing on a beach. I asked her about the sand; she talked for a long time; she didn't mention the phone once. When I finally turned the phone on, it was only for a short moment, to get the end of the story. She didn't seem to mind. The shift from extraction to connection made all the difference.

Start with a 10-minute "low-stakes" recording where you both just chat about a neutral topic (like a recent meal or a garden project). The goal is not to get a story, but to get comfortable with the sound of the recording playing back. This small, low-pressure step builds trust without demanding a narrative.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it rude to delete a recording if my mum gets upset?

No, it is a necessary act of respect. Deleting the specific segment that caused distress validates her boundary and rebuilds trust for future sessions. It shows that her comfort is more important than the completeness of the recording, which is essential for a healthy, ongoing dialogue.

### Can I record her without her knowing?

No; this violates trust and ethical guidelines. The process only works if it is a collaborative, consensual act. Secret recording creates a sense of surveillance rather than connection, and if she finds out, it will likely damage the relationship and make future conversations much harder.

### What if she says "I have nothing interesting to say"?

This is often a deflection of vulnerability. Respond with, "I don't need it to be interesting, I just need it to be yours. Even the small things matter to me." This reassures her that you value her perspective, not just dramatic events, and helps lower the pressure to perform.

### How long should a session be if she is resistant?

Keep it under 15 minutes. Short sessions reduce the feeling of being "trapped" in a conversation and make it easier to stop when she feels overwhelmed. It also makes the process feel less like a major commitment and more like a casual chat.

### Does she have to sign a consent form?

While not legally required for a private family gift, a verbal agreement on what will and won't be shared is essential for psychological safety. This verbal consent helps her feel in control and ensures that the recording is used in a way that respects her wishes.
