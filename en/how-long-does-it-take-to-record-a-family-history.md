# How Long Does It Take To Record A Family History?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/bb2b16b28499a07b182c.delivery-v1.webp?hero=ca1c6d67cabacb23>)

Recording a family history does not require months of dedicated time. By focusing on one core narrative arc rather than exhaustive detail, the core recording process can be completed in a single, focused session of 60–90 minutes, or broken into three 30-minute conversations over a week. The goal is capturing the essence of the story, not creating an archival database.

A common misconception is that recording a family history is a multi-year project. Many people have seen genealogy websites, read forums, and encountered figures suggesting hundreds of hours of work. This often leads to the belief that the task involves tracing lines back centuries and filling out endless pedigree charts. It can feel like a project that will take a year, or a lifetime, creating a sense of exhaustion before the process even begins.

That fear is understandable, but it is based on the wrong definition of the task. You are not building a database or verifying dates against census records; you are trying to capture [the voice and](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) the feeling of the person sitting in front of you. The time commitment is much smaller than the genealogy sites suggest, because it is not a months-long commitment but a conversation.

## How long does it actually take to record a parent’s life story?

The short answer is that the core recording process takes between 60 and 90 minutes. If you are worried about how long does it take to record a family history, the answer is usually one afternoon, or three short mornings.

This is a significant difference from the extensive time required for genealogical research. That type of work applies to tracing ancestors back to the 1500s using documents. Your goal is not to prove who your great-great-grandfather was. Your goal is to hear your parent describe their first job, their marriage, or the house they grew up in.

A single 60 to 90-minute session is sufficient to capture the core narrative of a life. You do not need to cover every year; you need to cover the theme.

## What is the difference between recording a story and building a family tree?

This is where the confusion usually starts; building a family tree is an archival task. It involves finding records, verifying relationships, and entering data into a software platform. It is precise, dry, and often tedious. It requires hours of research for every single name.

Recording a life story is a narrative task; it is about memory, emotion, and perspective. Your parent does not need to be factually perfect. They need to be present. If they remember the car as blue when it was actually green, that is the story you want. The feeling of the memory is more valuable than the technical accuracy of the data.

When you treat the task [as a story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-handle-it-when-my-grandparent-repeats-the-same-story-over-and>) rather than a database, the time pressure disappears. You are not trying to fill a spreadsheet; you are trying to have a chat.

## How can I break the process into 30-minute sessions?

If one hour feels like too much, you can split it. The most effective way to do this is to divide the life into three distinct 30-minute blocks.

1. **Early life:** Childhood, school, and the first few years of adulthood.
2. **Middle life:** Career, relationships, and the years of raising a family.
3. **Reflections:** Looking back, legacy, and what matters most now.

This structure fits easily into an Australian lifestyle. You can do the first block after dropping the kids at school. The second block can happen during a lunch break or a weekend morning. The third block can be a quiet evening chat.

Each session is short enough that neither of you gets tired. It also allows the story to breathe. You might find that the third session naturally picks up threads from the first, which is fine. The goal is a coherent narrative, not a chronological log. If you are unsure where to start, [how to start a life story conversation without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-start-a-life-story-conversation-without-it-feeling-like-an>) can help you find the right opening questions.

## What are the three essential parts of a life story?

You do not need to record every birthday, every holiday, or every job. That is the "commitment trap." It assumes that a life is a list of events. It is not; a life is a sequence of turning points.

The three essential parts are:

- **The Origin:** Where they came from and what shaped their early character.
- **The Journey:** The major choices they made and the paths they took.
- **The Meaning:** What they have learned and what they want to leave behind.

If you capture these three arcs, you have a complete story. The details will fill in the gaps; you don't need to force them.

## How much time should I spend on preparation versus recording?

Preparation should take 15 to 20 minutes; that is all. Pick three or five key questions that you are curious about. Write them down on a piece of paper; that is it.

Do not spend hours researching their life before the conversation. They are the expert on their own life; your job is to ask, not to audit.

The time you save here is significant. If you are doing this yourself, the post-processing can take 10 or more hours. Transcribing, editing, and formatting a 90-minute recording is a massive task.

## What happens if I run out of time or energy?

It is common for people to get tired, for memories to trigger emotions, and for the conversation to stall.

If you run out of time, stop; do not force it to the end. A 45-minute conversation is better than a 90-minute one where the last 30 minutes are flat.

If you run out of energy, take a break. Come back the next day, or the next week. The story is not going anywhere.

If the conversation becomes emotional, that is normal. It is a good sign that the story matters. You can pause, offer a tissue, or just sit in silence. For guidance on managing these moments, see what to do if my parent gets emotional and cries during the recording.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it like a research project.** You are not a historian; you are a family member. Stop looking for primary sources and start listening to the person.

**Trying to cover everything.** The desire to be comprehensive is the enemy of completion. A focused story is better than a scattered collection of facts.

**Waiting for the ideal moment.** A Tuesday afternoon with a cup of tea is enough.

**Doing it all in one go.** If you try to do it in one sitting, you will likely burn out, so break it up.

Ask permission before recording, let the person choose the pace, and pause if they become tired or distressed. [Dementia Australia guidance](<https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-connected/talking-someone-dementia>) explains person-centred ways to communicate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to record every single detail of their life?

No; focus on the key themes and turning points. A coherent narrative is more valuable than an exhaustive list of dates. You want to capture the essence of their experience, not create an archival database.

### What if my parent has dementia or memory loss?

Focus on their feelings and the present moment rather than factual accuracy. Shorter sessions of 15 to 20 minutes are often better. Follow their lead and stop if they become distressed.

### Is it too late to start if my parent is in their 80s?

No. The most important stories are often the ones they have lived through. The process is about capturing their perspective, not historical research. Their current understanding and feelings are the most valuable parts of the story.

### How long does it take to record a family history over video call?

The time commitment remains the same, typically 60 to 90 minutes total, regardless of distance. Video calls work well for 30-minute sessions, provided you have good lighting and audio. You only need to focus on the conversation, as the technical aspects of compiling the final product are separate from the recording itself.
