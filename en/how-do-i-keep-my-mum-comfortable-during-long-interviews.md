# How Do I Keep My Mum Comfortable During Long Interviews?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/8fe240f5540e83371e34.delivery-v1.webp?hero=9a16cc4f5335aa94>)

Keep your mum comfortable by treating the session as a conversation, not an interrogation. Respect her cognitive energy by watching for signs of fatigue, ensure she has water and a comfortable chair, and explicitly give her permission to stop or skip any topic. Prioritise her emotional state over completing the recording; if she shows signs of fatigue or distress, pause immediately and resume only when she is ready.

You are sitting across from your mum, the recorder is on, and you hear the distinct, wet sound of her swallowing. Her fingers are gripping the armrests of the chair tightly, knuckles whitening. She is trying to be helpful, trying to give you the story, but you can feel the effort it is taking her. You worry that you are pushing too hard, that you are turning a quiet afternoon into a performance, and that you might be causing her distress rather than capturing her joy.

You find yourself holding your breath, your eyes flicking to the clock on the wall to check how much time has passed. This physical tension in your own body is a signal. Many people treat life story recording like a journalistic task, where the goal is to extract as much information as possible in one go. But for an older adult, sustained verbal recall is often more taxing than physical movement. When you frame the session as a care-sensitive interaction rather than an interview, the pressure drops, and the conversation becomes something she can actually sustain.

The goal is not to finish the book in an afternoon. The goal is to keep her comfortable enough that she wants to come back tomorrow.

## Why do long interviews exhaust older adults so quickly?

Retrieving a memory is not a simple act of pulling a file from a cabinet. It is a complex reconstruction process. When your mum tries to recall a specific afternoon from decades ago, her brain must rebuild the scene, the emotions, and the sensory details from scratch. This requires significant cognitive energy. If you ask for a long, continuous narrative, you are asking her to perform a high-level mental task for an extended period, which depletes her resources much faster than a simple physical activity would.

Physical fatigue is obvious, but cognitive fatigue is subtle. It looks like shorter answers, a slight drift in topic, or a pause that lasts a bit longer than usual. If you push through this, you risk getting a flat, rushed recording that misses the nuance. By recognising this as a physiological limit rather than a lack of interest, you can adjust your approach to protect her energy.

## What is the ideal length for a single life story session?

There is no single rule that fits every person. Instead, watch her specific energy levels to determine the natural end point. If she is still talking and seems energised, you can gently extend the conversation. If she seems to be winding down, end it before she does. Leaving her wanting a little more is a much better place to be than leaving her exhausted.

Think of it as a conversation over a cup of tea, not a formal deposition. The length of the session should be dictated by her engagement, not by a clock. If she is losing focus, it is time to stop, regardless of how much time has passed.

## How do I structure breaks so they don’t feel like interruptions?

Use natural anchors. In Australia, the specific sound of the kettle boiling and the ritual of placing a slice of Tim Tam on a saucer are powerful comfort markers. When you notice her energy dipping, suggest a break for a cup of tea. This is not a stop; it is a reset. It gives her a moment to breathe, hydrate, and shift her focus.

You can also use the environment as a break marker. If you are sitting in a room with air conditioning, the dry air can be dehydrating, especially in winter. If it is summer, the heat can be draining. Moving to a different spot, or simply standing up to stretch, can help reset the physical comfort level. These small shifts keep the session feeling like a shared activity rather than a task.

## What physical environment changes reduce fatigue during recording?

Start with the chair. A firm dining chair is often better than a soft armchair for these sessions. A soft armchair can cause her to slump, which restricts breathing and leads to physical fatigue and discomfort. A firm chair supports an upright posture, which helps her stay alert and engaged without the physical strain of sinking into cushions.

Regarding equipment, resist the urge to use professional lavaliere mics if they make her self-conscious. A phone placed on the table is often better. It is less intrusive, less technical, and feels more like a casual conversation. The lower the pressure of the setup, the more natural her voice will be.

## How do I handle it if my mum starts to drift or repeat herself?

If she starts repeating a sentence, or if her answers become vague, do not correct her or try to steer her back. Instead, physically stop the recorder. This action signals a pause without the pressure of continuing. Then, use a gentle script: "Mum, you don’t have to answer that. Let’s just sit for a minute."

This gives her permission to stop without feeling like she has failed. It also allows you to assess her state. If she is just tired, a short break will help. If she is emotionally drained, you might need to end the session. If you are struggling to keep the conversation going because she is giving short answers, it is often a sign of fatigue or disengagement, and you might find it helpful to look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>).

## What people get wrong

**Treating it as a performance.** If she feels she is being judged on her memory, she will shut down, so keep the tone light and curious rather than evaluative.

**Ignoring the physical signs.** Slumped posture, shorter answers, and repeating sentences are not just quirks; they are signals that you must respect.

**Pushing for more detail.** If she is getting emotional or tired, stopping is the right call. You can always ask the question again another day. If you are unsure how to handle the emotional side, [what to do if my grandparent gets emotional during a life story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-do-if-my-grandparent-gets-emotional-during-a-life-story>) offers more guidance.

**Forgetting the "why".** Remind yourself why you are doing this, which is to preserve her voice rather than just fill a notebook.

You will likely have several short, broken-up sessions rather than one long one. This is normal, and it is actually better for the quality of the story. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I keep my mum comfortable during long interviews?

To keep your mum comfortable, treat the session as a casual conversation rather than a formal interview. Watch for signs of cognitive fatigue, such as shorter answers or slumped posture, and pause immediately if you see them. Ensure she has water and a comfortable chair, and explicitly give her permission to stop or skip any topic. Prioritising her emotional state over completing the recording ensures the interaction remains warm and manageable.

### How do I know if my mum is too tired to continue?

Look for signs like slumped posture, shorter answers, or repeating the same sentence. If you see these, stop immediately; it is better to end early than to push her.

### Should I record in one long session or multiple short ones?

Multiple short ones are significantly better for preserving quality and comfort. Long sessions often lead to "rambling" or loss of detail.

### What if she falls asleep during the interview?

This is a sign of extreme fatigue, so stop recording, cover her with a blanket, and do not try to wake her up. Schedule the next session for a different day.

### Is it okay to stop the recording if she gets upset?

Yes; always prioritise her emotional well-being over the content. You can always ask the question again another day when she is in a better headspace.

### Do I need to ask her permission to stop?

You can simply say, "I think we’ve done enough for today," and stop. You don’t need a formal permission ritual, but a gentle suggestion to end is respectful.
