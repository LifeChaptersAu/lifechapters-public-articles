# What To Ask A Grandparent About Their Daily Routine?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/14eb51404db3e29eb379.delivery-v1.webp?hero=a27ef518a1bd2e35>)

Ask about the specific sensory details of their morning. What is the first thing they touch? What do they smell when they open the kitchen door? What is the one task they refuse to let anyone else do? These mundane, repetitive actions reveal their values, independence, and the quiet rhythms that define their personality more than any major life event.

A common approach to life stories focuses on major milestones. The wedding, the war, the move to Australia. These events are important, but they often lack the specific texture of a person’s daily existence. A generic account of a wedding might mention the dress and the venue, but it rarely captures the specific anxiety of finding the right shoes or the exact taste of the cake. In contrast, a specific detail of a morning routine, such as the precise temperature of the water for tea or the order in which the curtains are opened, reveals the individual’s unique preferences and habits. These small, repetitive actions are the layer of a person that is disappearing in the quiet hours of a Tuesday afternoon.

The purpose of this article is to help you understand who a person is today. It addresses the concern of missing the past or asking the wrong questions. The goal is not to write a biography, but to understand the present. This is a different, and often more intimate, task.

The problem is that we are taught to ask about history, but we forget to ask about the present, and it is in the present that the personality lives.

## Why do the "boring" parts of a day reveal the most about a person?

Major life events are often told in the same way, because they are part of a shared narrative. For example, many people describe the "hard times" of the 1970s as a period of general hardship, often citing high inflation or job insecurity, without specific personal details. This shared narrative makes the story feel generic. In contrast, how someone makes their tea, or how they arrange the cushions on their favourite chair, is unique to them.

These small habits reveal independence, pride, and the specific way a person has learned to make a house feel like home. Asking about these "boring" parts treats their current existence as valuable, not just their past achievements. It shifts the focus from looking back to looking at, and that shift changes the entire tone of the conversation.

## What should you ask a grandparent about their morning routine to capture their voice?

The morning is the best place to start because it is routine, safe, and does not require them to dig up memories of pain or loss. It is just the day beginning.

Try asking about the sensory details. Instead of "What do you do in the morning?", ask:

- What is the first sound you hear when you wake up?
- What does the kitchen smell like at 7 AM?
- What is the first thing you touch?

These questions are low-pressure. They are not asking for a story, they are asking for an observation. And because they are so specific, they often lead to a much deeper conversation about how they have always liked things to be.

For example, if they mention the sound of the mailman, you might find out they have been listening for that specific sound for fifty years. That is a detail no biography would ever capture, but it tells you everything about their attention to the world.

## How can you discuss their daily habits without it feeling like an interrogation?

The biggest mistake is treating the conversation like a job interview. If you are sitting with a notepad, ticking off questions, they will feel like a subject, not a person.

The goal is a chat. You are sitting together, maybe having a cup of tea, and you are curious about their day. If a question feels like a demand, drop it. If they give a short answer, let it go. The conversation should feel like it is happening naturally, not like it is being extracted.

This is where the "refusal" test comes in. Ask them: "Is there one small task you insist on doing yourself, even if you don't have to?"

This is a powerful question because it reveals their sense of independence. For many grandparents, making their own tea, or watering their own plants, is not just a chore, it is a way of saying "I am still here, I am still capable." Asking about this with respect, rather than surprise, can be very moving.

## Which specific sensory questions open up memories of their current home life?

The home is a character in their life; it has a smell, a light, a sound. Asking about the home in the present tense can open up memories that are not about the past, but about the feeling of being there now.

Try these:

- What is your favourite chair, and why that one?
- What time of day does the light come in best?
- What is the one thing you never let anyone else touch?

These questions are about the present, but they often lead to the past. They might tell you why they bought that chair, or how the light used to be in their old house. But the anchor is the present; the home is still there; the light is still coming in.

This is a gentle way to explore their life without forcing them to look back. It lets them talk about what they see and feel right now.

## What should you ask when they give short answers about their day?

Sometimes, no matter how you ask, the answers are short. "I had a cup of tea." "I watched the telly." "I sat in the garden."

This is not a failure, but rather a reflection of who they are or how they are feeling that day, so do not push or try to get them to "open up."

Instead, try a different angle. Ask about the "why" behind the "what." If they say they watched the telly, ask what they were watching. If they say they sat in the garden, ask what they were looking at.

You can also try the "micro-routine" approach; ask about the small, repetitive actions. What do they do with the remote control? How do they hold their cup? These small details are often easier to talk about than big, abstract questions.

## What people get wrong

**Asking about the past instead of the present.** "What did you do in the morning when you were young?" is a history question; "What do you do in the morning now?" is a personality question; the present is where the life is.

**Treating it like a project.** If you are writing down every answer, you are editing the conversation. Let it flow; you can always ask again later.

**Ignoring the sensory details.** "How was your day?" is too big; "What did you smell?" is specific; specificity is what makes a conversation feel real.

**Forcing a story.** Not every day has a narrative; sometimes a day is just a day; that is okay. The value is in the texture, not the plot.

## What does a quiet afternoon in the kitchen look like?

A common example of this type of conversation takes place in a kitchen on a Tuesday afternoon. The light is coming in through the window, and there is a cup of tea on the table. The grandparent is not telling you about their wedding, or their job, or their children. They are telling you about the way the light hits the floorboards at 3 PM. They are telling you about the sound of the kettle boiling. They are telling you about the specific way they stir their tea, three times, always.

This is the kind of conversation that stays with you. It is not a story you can retell at a dinner party. It is a feeling you can hold.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I ask about their daily routine without making them feel old?

Focus on the sensory and skill aspects, such as how they make their tea or arrange their space, rather than the age aspects. Frame it as learning a preference or a habit, not documenting decline. This keeps the conversation respectful and centred on their current capabilities.

### What if my grandparent says they do nothing all day?

Ask for the "micro-routines." What do they watch? What do they listen to? What is their favourite chair? These small anchors are the building blocks of their day. Even if they feel uneventful, they are the texture of their life.

### Is it okay to record their daily routine if they are tired?

Only if they are comfortable. If they are tired, it is better to ask one or two specific questions and stop. Comfort and permission always take priority over completing a list. You can always come back another day.

### Which daily routine questions are best for a grandparent with dementia?

Focus on the present moment and sensory details, such as smells, sounds, and tastes, rather than chronological history. Keep questions simple and open-ended. Consult Dementia Australia for specific communication strategies tailored to their needs.

### How do I turn these routine questions into a story?

Look for the "why" behind the "what." If they always check the stove, ask why that matters to them. The answer reveals their values, such as safety, responsibility, or love. The story is in the meaning, not just the action.
