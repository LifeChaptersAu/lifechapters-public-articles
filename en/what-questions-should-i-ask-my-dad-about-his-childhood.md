# What Questions Should I Ask My Dad About His Childhood?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/f8c468e25d1423f27d06.delivery-v1.webp>)

Ask about specific things, not feelings, such as the house he grew up in, room by room, what he did between school and dinner, what his father did for work, or what he got in trouble for. Concrete questions open memory, while abstract ones get "it was fine". Then, every time he stops, ask what happened next.

The television is low in the background, and you are holding a mug that has gone lukewarm while you listen. You want to know his childhood, but you are worried you have waited too long, or that if you ask the wrong thing, he will shut down.

This is not about extracting a biography; it is about getting him to talk. Dads are often the harder parent to open up because they were raised to be stoic, to fix things, and to not complain. When you ask a big, open question, they give you the safe, flat answer. "It was fine." "Pretty typical."

You do not need to be a journalist; you just need to ask smaller questions.

## What are the ten questions to ask my dad about his childhood?

Start with the physical world, as memory is anchored in place and object rather than abstract time. These ten questions are ordered from easy to deep, moving from the concrete to the felt.

1. Walk me through the house you grew up in — start at the front door.
2. What did you do between getting home from school and dinner?
3. Who did you knock about with? Are they still around?
4. What did your dad do for work? What did he smell like when he got home?
5. What were you in trouble for most?
6. What did your mum cook on a Sunday?
7. What did you have that no kid has now?
8. What was the first thing you ever bought with your own money?
9. What were you frightened of?
10. What's something from back then you've never told me?

Do not try to ask all ten in one go. Pick one, and let it breathe.

## Where do I start if we've never talked like this before?

Do not schedule a sit-down, as a chair, a table, and a phone on the surface creates an interview, and interviews make people perform.

Instead, use side-by-side conversation, which removes the pressure of eye contact. You are both doing something else, such as washing up together, working in the shed, or driving to the shops. Hands are busy, and the pressure to be profound disappears.

If you are not together physically, a phone call works, but only if it is casual. Do not say, "I have some questions for you." Say, "I was just thinking about the old house, do you remember the kitchen?"

Another strong lever is photos. If you have old photos, hold one up and ask, "What's the story behind this one?" This is a standard practice for a reason, as the photo provides the anchor. He does not have to search for a memory; he is just describing what he sees.

## What do I say when he answers in three words?

The conversation often ends here because the question was too big. If you ask, "What were your teenage years like?" and he says, "Pretty typical," the exchange stops. You have asked a question that invites a summary, and he has given you the summary.

You need to shrink the question. You need a follow-up line that forces him back into the scene. Here are four scripts that work:

- **"What happened next?"** This bypasses the need for summarization. It moves him from a static fact to a narrative.
- **"Who else was there?"** This turns a fact into a scene with people in it.
- **"What did that look like?"** This moves him from summary to picture.
- **Say nothing for five seconds.** Most people fill a silence. If he gives a short answer, wait. Count to five in your head. Often, they will add the detail you actually wanted.

If he is genuinely closed off, or if these lines feel forced, you might need a different approach to opening the door. ## What should I ask about his own father?

This is often the most revealing part of a dad's childhood. He was a son before he was a dad, and asking about his father gives you insight into where his own values came from.

Do not ask, "What was your dad like?" That is a character judgment, and he will give you the polished version.

Ask about the work. "What did your dad do for work?" "What did he smell like when he got home?" "Did he talk about work at the dinner table?"

Ask about the rules. "What were you not allowed to do?" "Who was the strictest?"

These questions bypass the emotional filter. They go straight to the daily reality of his childhood.

## What questions work for a dad who grew up in Australia in the 1950s or 60s?

Australian childhoods have specific textures. Use these as prompts, as they are not historical assertions but hooks to see if they land.

- **The backyard.** Was there cricket? A trampoline? A shed?
- **The food.** Fish and chips wrapped in newspaper? The milk bar? The corner shop?
- **The events.** The changeover to decimal currency in 1966. The Melbourne Olympics in 1956 and whether his family had a television or watched at a neighbour's. The moon landing in 1969.
- **The migration.** If his family came out after the war, ask about the boat, the temporary accommodation, and the first house. What did his parents speak at home versus at the shop?

If you are unsure about the timing of specific cultural events like cracker night, just ask, "Did you have cracker nights?" Let him confirm or correct you.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it as an interview.** A list plus a chair plus a phone on the table equals an interrogation. It is a conversation you are having anyway, with better questions.

**Asking about feelings first.** "How did that make you feel?" as an opener closes most men down, because feelings arrive on their own, three questions after the concrete one.

**Chasing the big questions.** People go straight for regrets, legacy, "what do you want to be remembered for". Those answers are performances. The story about the bike he saved for is the real thing.

**Trying to do it all in one go.** A single long session often captures less detail than several shorter conversations spread over time. The longer the session, the more likely the memory becomes compressed or generalized rather than specific.

## How do I record it without it feeling like an interview?

You do not need a studio; you need a phone.

Before you start, open the voice recorder app. Put the phone on the table, or in your pocket. Tell him, "I'm just going to record this so I don't forget what you say." This is honest. It signals that you value what he is saying.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I ask my dad for permission before recording his story?

Yes, always ask for permission before you start recording. A simple, casual request like "I'm just going to record this so I don't forget what you say" is usually enough. It shows respect for his privacy and makes the moment feel collaborative rather than like an interrogation.

### What if my dad gets tired or distracted during the conversation?

That is completely normal, especially for older adults. If he seems tired or distracted, gently pause the conversation and suggest picking it up another time. You do not need to finish everything in one sitting; shorter, more frequent chats often yield better memories than one long session.

### How do I handle it if my dad says he has nothing interesting to share?

This is a common response, so do not take it personally. Instead of arguing, pivot to a specific, concrete question about a place or object he knows well, like the house he grew up in. Concrete details are easier to recall than abstract feelings, and they often spark a chain of memories he did not realise he had.

### What if my dad is reluctant to talk about his own father?

This is often the most sensitive part of the conversation. If he seems hesitant, do not push. You can gently ask about his father's work or daily habits, which are less emotionally charged than character judgments. If he still seems closed off, it is okay to leave the topic for another time and revisit it when the conversation feels more comfortable.

### How long should each conversation be to keep his attention?

Keep sessions short, around 20 to 30 minutes, to avoid fatigue and maintain his engagement. If he seems to lose focus or becomes quiet, it is a good time to wrap up and suggest another chat later. Shorter, more frequent conversations often lead to more detailed and relaxed storytelling than one long, exhausting session.
