# How Do I Keep A Dementia Interview Gentle And Short?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/5f7811b143dc1c7865df.delivery-v1.webp?hero=d389ee13ebcc9f91>)

Keep the session to 10–15 minutes, ending before frustration sets in. Use sensory anchors like photos or objects to trigger specific memories, ask open-ended questions about feelings rather than facts, and stop while the conversation is still positive. This respects cognitive fatigue and preserves dignity over exhaustive data collection.

You are sitting at the table, the smell of burnt toast hanging in the air, a notebook open in front of you. You can feel the weight of the task. You want to capture the story, but you are also watching for the signs that they are getting tired, or that the question has landed badly. It is a delicate balance, and it is easy to feel like you are failing if the conversation stalls.

You are not failing. You are just trying to do too much at once. The goal is not to extract every detail of their life in one sitting; that is a recipe for exhaustion for both of you. Instead, the goal is to create a small, safe space where a memory can surface naturally, and then to let it go.

This approach is about protecting the relationship first. The story is important, but the person in front of you is more important. If you keep the sessions short and the questions gentle, you are more likely to get the authentic moments that matter, rather than a strained performance.

## Why does a 10-minute limit actually help my parent with dementia?

Short sessions respect the way memory works in dementia. Mental energy depletes quickly, and pushing past that point often leads to frustration or withdrawal. By limiting the time, you are removing the pressure to perform.

Ten to fifteen minutes is a common observation for maintaining engagement, allowing enough time to connect, share one or two moments, and end on a high note. It also makes it easier to return to the topic another day. You are not trying to solve the whole puzzle in one go; you are just adding one piece. This reduces the anxiety for you, too, because you are not carrying the weight of a "final" interview.

## How do I choose the right sensory anchors to start a conversation?

Abstract questions like "Tell me about your childhood" are too big. They require the person to search through years of memory, which is cognitively expensive. Instead, use a sensory anchor. This is a physical object or a specific detail that grounds the conversation in the present moment.

A wedding ring, a recipe card, or a single photograph works well. Hold it up, or place it on the table, and ask a simple question about it. "What did you buy with your first pay packet?" or "Who was this person?" These questions are easier to answer because they are tied to a concrete thing. The object does the heavy lifting, and the memory follows.

## What specific questions avoid triggering frustration or repetition?

Avoid questions that require complex recall or that can be answered with a simple yes or no. Instead, ask about feelings and specific moments. "How did it feel when you arrived?" is better than "When did you arrive?" because it invites a narrative rather than a date.

If you find yourself stuck, or if they are giving short answers, it might help to look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>). The key is to follow their lead. If they light up about a particular topic, stay there. If they seem to drift, gently bring them back to the object in front of you.

## How do I know when it is time to stop the session?

Watch for non-verbal cues. Fidgeting, sighing, or repeating the same phrase are signs that their mental energy is running low. You do not need to wait for them to say they are tired; they may not even realise it.

If you see these signs, it is time to stop. You can say something simple like, "I'm going to let you rest now," or "Let's have a cup of tea." Ending the session while the conversation is still positive leaves them feeling good about the interaction, rather than drained. If they become emotional, you can find more guidance on [what to do if my grandparent gets emotional during a life story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-do-if-my-grandparent-gets-emotional-during-a-life-story>).

## What if my parent repeats the same story or gets stuck?

Repetition is a common part of dementia, and it does not mean you are failing. It often means that the story is important to them, or that they are seeking comfort. Use the "Validate, Join, Distract" method. Validate their feeling ("I can see that was a happy time"), join in briefly ("That sounds wonderful"), and then gently distract or redirect to a new anchor.

Do not correct them. If they say the wrong year, or the wrong name, let it go. You are not fact-checking; you are connecting. Correcting them can feel like a rejection, and it shuts down the conversation. Your job is to be present, not to be accurate.

## How do I handle my own guilt about not getting "enough" information?

This is the hardest part. You want to capture everything, but you also know that pushing for more can hurt them. The guilt comes from the fear that you are missing something important. But the truth is that the feeling of the moment is more valuable than the facts.

If you capture the joy, the love, or the pride in their voice, you have captured the essence. The dates and details are less important than the spirit. Let go of the need for a complete record. A few small, genuine moments are far more powerful than a long, strained interview.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it like an interview.** The word "interview" implies a power dynamic, so you are not a journalist but a family member. Sit at the same eye level, remove background noise like the TV, and keep the tone casual. If it feels like a performance, they will perform. If it feels like a chat, they will talk. You can learn more about [how to record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>) to shift your mindset.

**Pushing for a perfect take.** If you are recording, it is okay to pause or stop if they show signs of stress. Do not try to get a "clean" recording. The pauses, the sighs, the repetitions are part of the humanity. A rough recording made with care is better than a perfect one made under pressure.

**Ignoring their comfort.** If they are tired, stop; if they are upset, stop. Your goal is to connect, not to extract data. Their comfort is the priority.

**Waiting for the right time.** A quiet afternoon is a suitable time to begin. You do not need to wait for a special occasion. A cup of tea, a single photo; these small moments are where the connection happens.

Preserving the voice for the family is the primary goal, ensuring that the tone and emotion of the conversation remain intact for those who will listen later.

For clinical or consent-capacity questions, speak with a doctor or qualified health professional. [Dementia Australia guidance](<https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-connected/talking-someone-dementia>) explains person-centred ways to communicate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a dementia interview session be?

10 to 15 minutes is ideal to respect cognitive fatigue. Quality over quantity is key, as shorter sessions help maintain their dignity and prevent mental exhaustion.

### What if my parent keeps repeating the same story?

Validate the emotion behind the story, join in briefly, and gently redirect to a new sensory anchor or activity. Repetition is often a sign of comfort or importance, not a failure of memory.

### Is it okay to record if my parent has early-stage dementia?

Yes, but keep sessions short, ensure they are comfortable, and prioritize their dignity over the recording. Always ask for permission and stop if they show signs of stress.

### How do I stop feeling guilty about not getting more details?

Remember that capturing the feeling and spirit of a moment is more valuable than exhaustive factual data. The connection you share is what matters most.

### What should I do if my parent gets frustrated?

Stop immediately, offer a break, and shift to a calming activity like holding hands or looking at photos together. Their comfort is more important than completing the session.
