# What To Ask Dad Who Grew Up In The 1960s?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/a5c2e67c8c24e9623114.delivery-v1.webp?hero=b245001a460b10f9>)

Ask about specific sensory details from the 1960s, such as the smell of petrol in his first car, the static on a black-and-white TV, or the local shop where he bought his first shoes. These concrete anchors bypass the abstract "tell me about your childhood" prompt that usually leads to silence.

You sit down with your dad, and the air smells faintly of the petrol and chrome that clung to his first Holden, a scent that defined the era. You ask him about his childhood; he looks at you, maybe smiles, and says, "It was a good time." Then the conversation stalls. You feel a bit foolish, like you asked the wrong thing, but you can't quite put your finger on why.

This silence is often the result of the specific cultural pressure of 1960s Australian masculinity, which demanded that men handle problems quietly and keep emotions private. Asking a broad question like "tell me about your life" asks them to summarize decades of unspoken feelings, which is an impossible task. They don't have the words for it, so they give you the safe, generic answer.

You don't need to be a psychologist or a professional interviewer to get past this. You just need to change the type of question you ask. Instead of asking about feelings or broad eras, ask about specific, sensory moments. These are the details that bypass the emotional filter and tap directly into memory.

## Why does "Tell me about your childhood" fail with a 1960s dad?

A man who grew up in the 1960s was likely taught that his feelings were his own business. The cultural norm was to "toughen up" and handle things without complaint. When you ask a broad question, you are asking him to perform an emotional narrative, which feels vulnerable and unsafe.

He doesn't have a ready-made story to tell you because he never had to articulate his inner life to anyone. He lived it, but he didn't write it down or talk about it. So when you ask for the story, he reaches for the only thing he has: a summary. "It was a good time" is not a lie; it is a shield. It protects him from having to dig up memories that might be painful or complicated.

To get past the shield, you need to ask about the world around him, not the feelings inside him. The world is factual and safe, and once he starts talking about it, the feelings often come out on their own because they are attached to the memories.

## What should you ask about the sensory world of the 1960s?

Sensory details are the key because they are specific, concrete, and easy to remember, allowing them to bypass the emotional labour required to access abstract feelings.

Try asking about smells. What did the house smell like on a Sunday morning? Did it smell of coffee, or maybe the specific brand of detergent his mother used, such as Rinso or Persil? These smells are powerful triggers for memory.

Ask about sounds. What did the radio sound like in the kitchen? Was there a specific song that played every week? What did the static on the black-and-white TV set sound like when they changed the channel?

Ask about tastes. What was the first meal he cooked for himself? What did he eat for breakfast on a school day? Was it toast with jam, or maybe a specific brand of cereal?

These questions are low-stakes. They are not asking him to judge his life or explain his feelings. They are just asking him to describe a moment. And once he starts describing the moment, the story often unfolds naturally.

## How can you access memories of 1960s Australian social life?

The 1960s in Australia had a distinct social texture, exemplified by the national shift to decimal currency in 1966, which changed how men managed their daily finances and purchases.

For example, the introduction of decimal currency was a major event. Ask him what he thought about the change. Did he have a specific coin or note that he kept? What was the first thing he bought with the new money?

The 1962 Commonwealth Games were another big event. Did he watch the opening ceremony? Did he know anyone who was involved in the games? These are specific, factual questions that can open up a door to broader memories about that time.

You can also ask about the social norms of the time. The phrase "Wait until your father gets home" was a common way to discipline children. Ask him if he heard that phrase, and what it meant to him. Was it a threat, or just a fact of life? This can lead to a conversation about his parents and the family dynamic.

## What should you ask about work and independence in the 1960s?

Men of the 1960s generation were often expected to be independent and self-reliant, a trait forged in trades like auto mechanics, where they learned to fix complex machinery with their own hands and not complain about the difficulty.

Ask him about his first job. What did he do? What did the workplace smell like? Was it a factory, an office, or a shop? What was the first tool he used?

Ask him about his first car. What was the make and model? Was it a Holden or a Ford? What did the petrol smell like? Did he have to wash it by hand? These are specific, tangible details that he can remember easily.

Ask him about the expectation of independence. Did his parents tell him to "figure it out" when he had a problem? How did he learn to solve problems on his own? This can lead to a conversation about his resilience and his values.

## What people get wrong

**Asking too many questions at once.** This can feel like an interrogation, so stick to one or two questions per conversation and let the silence do some of the work.

**Pushing for emotions.** If he gives a short answer, don't push him to explain why he feels that way. Let the facts speak for themselves. The emotions will come if they are going to come.

**Correcting his memories.** If he says something that is factually wrong, let it go. You are not trying to create a historical record; you are trying to understand his experience. His memory is the truth for him.

**Assuming he doesn't have anything to say.** He does, but he just needs the right key to access it. ## How do you keep the conversation going when he gives short answers?

If he answers with a monosyllable and then falls silent, the conversation has stalled. This is not a failure on your part; it is a natural part of the process.

If this happens, you can try a different angle. Instead of asking a new question, you can comment on what he just said. "That sounds like a tough time," or "I can imagine that was exciting." This validates his experience and invites him to share more.

You can also use the "Do You Know?" technique. Asking specific "Do You Know?" questions about family history helps children feel more connected to their family. You can use this technique with your dad. "Do you know what your first car was?" or "Do you know what your mother used to cook for Sunday dinner?" These questions are specific and personal, and they often lead to richer answers.

If you are struggling to keep the conversation going, you might find it helpful to look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best question to ask dad who grew up in the 1960s?

Start with a specific sensory anchor like the smell of petrol in his first car or the static on a black-and-white TV. These concrete details bypass the emotional filter that broad questions trigger, allowing him to share real stories without feeling exposed.

### Is it too late to ask these questions if he is in his 70s?

No, it is not too late. Specific sensory details from the 1960s remain accessible at any age, and asking about concrete moments like his first car or local shop can still trigger vivid memories and meaningful connections.

### Should I record the conversation?

Only with his explicit permission. Some fathers feel more comfortable speaking freely if they know it is not being recorded.

### What if he gets emotional?

Pause and let him lead the conversation. If he wants to stop, respect that boundary, because the goal is genuine connection and shared understanding rather than extracting specific data or completing a list of questions.

### Are there specific 1960s Australian events I should mention?

Yes, the introduction of decimal currency (1966) and the 1962 Commonwealth Games are strong anchors for Australian men of that era.
