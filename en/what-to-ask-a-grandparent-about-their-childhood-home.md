# What To Ask A Grandparent About Their Childhood Home?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/33cc3e631189264a5d91.delivery-v1.webp?hero=5c360cf97f848158>)

Ask about the specific smells, sounds, and textures of the rooms, not just the layout. Instead of "What did the house look like?", ask "What did the kitchen smell like on Sunday mornings?" or "What sound did the floorboards make when you ran down the stairs?" These sensory anchors bypass the brain's tendency to generalize and bring back the vivid, specific memories that form the core of a life story.

You have probably tried asking, "What was your childhood home like?" and received a flat description of the number of bedrooms, or perhaps a look toward the ceiling and a simple, "It was a normal house." It is a frustrating moment, because you know there is a whole world in there, a world of dust motes and specific smells and the particular way the light came through the curtains, but it will not come out with that question.

You are looking for the feeling of being small in that house, the safety of it, or the fear, or the boredom. You want the story, not the floor plan.

The problem is not that they do not remember. It is that "what did it look like" is a question that asks for a summary. Summaries are safe, but they are also empty. To get the real story, you have to ask for the details that the brain stores separately from the facts.

## Why "What did your house look like?" is the wrong question to ask

When you ask for the look, you are asking for a visual snapshot, but memory is not a camera; it is a sensory experience. A person can forget the colour of the walls, but they will rarely forget the smell of the cooking, the sound of the radio, or the texture of the carpet under their feet.

Asking for smells bypasses the need to reconstruct a scene from logic. By asking for the sensory, you are inviting them to re-experience a moment, which is effortless and often joyful.

This is why the question "What did the kitchen smell like on Sunday mornings?" works so well. It does not ask them to describe a room. It asks them to go back to a specific time, a specific day, and a specific sense. The rest of the room usually follows once they are standing in it again.

## How do I use the five senses to bring back spatial memories?

You just need to move your questions away from the visual and into the other four senses. Here are some specific prompts that work well in an Australian context, where the home was often a weatherboard house with a veranda, or a brick home with a specific garden.

- **Smell:** "What did the air smell like when you opened the back door in summer?" or "Did the hallway have a particular scent, like polish or dust?"
- **Sound:** "What sound did the floorboards make when you ran down the stairs?" or "What was on the radio when you were eating breakfast?"
- **Texture:** "What was the texture of the wallpaper in the hallway?" or "What did the blankets feel like on a cold morning?"
- **Taste:** "What did you usually have for afternoon tea?" or "Did the water taste different in that house?"

These questions are specific enough to bypass the "I don't know" response. They give the brain a handle to grab onto. If they mention the smell of eucalyptus from the gum trees outside, that is a thread you can pull on. Ask about the trees; ask about the shade they provided; ask about the birds that nested in them.

## What should I ask about the routines and rituals of daily life?

The 4pm quiet after school is often more vivid than the layout of the rooms. The routines of a childhood home are often the most vivid parts of the memory because they were repeated so many times. Asking about the rhythm of the day can bring back more detail than asking about the structure of the house.

Try asking about specific times of day; "What did you do between 4 and 5 pm when the sun went down?" is a powerful question. It implies a specific time, a specific light, and a specific activity. It might lead to a story about playing in the yard, or waiting for dinner, or the particular quiet that settled over the house at that hour.

You can also ask about the rituals. "What did you do on a Saturday morning?" or "How did the family get ready for school?" These questions tap into the shared experiences that defined their childhood. They are less about the house as an object and more about the house as a container for their life.

## How do I handle the emotional weight of a home that no longer exists?

Sometimes, the home was not a happy place. Or it may have been lost, or sold, or fallen into disrepair. Asking about it can bring up grief, or anger, or a sense of loss. This is normal, and it does not mean you are doing something wrong.

If the house was sold after a divorce, the memory of the empty hallway may carry more weight than the garden. In this case, it is okay to pause. You do not need to fix it or solve it. You just need to acknowledge it. You might say, "That sounds like a difficult time," or "I can see that meant a lot to you."

If the conversation becomes too much, gently pivot to something lighter, or end the session. You can always come back to it another day. It is important to remember that you are not trying to extract a trauma narrative. You are trying to understand their life. If a part of that life is painful, that is part of the truth, but it does not have to be the whole story. ## What people get wrong

**Asking for a list.** "Tell me about the rooms" is a request for a list. Lists are dry. They do not have a beginning, middle, or end. They are just data; ask for a moment, not a list.

**Correcting the details.** If they say the kitchen was blue, and you know it was green, let it go. You are not building an architectural record; you are building a memory; their memory is the truth for this story. Arguing over the colour of the walls will shut them down.

**Rushing.** Do not try to cover the whole house in one sitting. Pick one room, or one time of day, and go deep. A rich, detailed story about the kitchen is far more valuable than a shallow tour of the entire house.

**Ignoring the silence.** If they pause, wait; do not jump in to fill the silence. Often, the most important details come after a pause, when they have found the right word.

## How do I turn these specific memories into a coherent narrative?

You will now have a collection of sensory details, routines, and moments. They may feel scattered, like a pile of photos without a story. This is where the work of structuring begins.

You can organise these memories by room, or by time of day, or by theme. For example, you might have a section on "The Kitchen," which includes the smell of the cooking, the sound of the radio, and the taste of the afternoon tea. This creates a natural flow that helps the reader visualize the space.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What to ask a grandparent about their childhood home if they have dementia?

Focus on the present moment and sensory experiences rather than factual recall. Ask about the smell of a specific food or the sound of a familiar song, as these often trigger immediate, positive responses. Keep the conversation short and supportive, and follow their lead if they become tired or distracted.

### How long should the conversation be?

Keep it short, around 20-30 minutes, to avoid fatigue. It is better to have several short conversations than one long, exhausting one.

### Should I record the conversation?

Yes, if your grandparent is comfortable with it. Recording allows you to capture the tone, pauses, and emotions that are lost in written notes.

### What if the home was associated with negative experiences?

Approach the topic with sensitivity. Allow your grandparent to set the boundaries and stop the conversation if it becomes too distressing.

### How do I organize these memories for a book?

Group them by room, time of day, or specific events. This creates a natural flow and helps the reader visualize the space.
