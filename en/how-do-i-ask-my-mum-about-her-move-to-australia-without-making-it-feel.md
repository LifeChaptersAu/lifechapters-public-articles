# How Do I Ask My Mum About Her Move To Australia?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/276f9f9adadfe913a722.delivery-v1.webp?hero=53002709491b6a7b>)

You don't need to ask for the "full story" in one go. Instead, anchor the conversation in a specific, sensory detail—like a recipe, a song, or a photo—and let her lead. By focusing on a small, safe fragment of the past, you give her permission to share as much or as little as she feels comfortable with, turning a potential interrogation into a shared moment of connection.

You are sitting across from your mum, the sharp scent of cumin and frying onions rising from the pan she is tending, and you want to know about the journey that brought her to Australia. But you are afraid. You are afraid that asking will feel like an interrogation, or that you will invalidate the sacrifices she made, or that you will trigger a pain she has kept quiet for decades.

This is a very common worry. The "move" is often a foundational event, yet it is rarely discussed openly. In many families, there is an unspoken rule about not discussing money or politics, and the migration story often falls into that same zone of silence. That silence can feel like a wall; you want to break through it, but gently; you want to connect, not to extract.

You do not need to be a journalist. You do not need a list of twenty questions. You just need to change the shape of the conversation.

## Why does asking about the "big story" feel like an interrogation?

When you ask, "Tell me about your journey to Australia," you are asking for a summary. You are asking her to compress years of emotion, logistics, and loss into a few minutes of speech. That is a heavy load. It feels like a performance, and she may feel judged or exposed.

This is a common concern, even when the relationship is close. You might find it helpful to look at [how to record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>), because the fear of making it feel like a formal interview is universal, regardless of gender or family dynamic.

The fix is to stop asking for the story and start asking for the details. Details are safe; stories are heavy.

## What is the difference between curiosity and prying?

Curiosity is about connection. It is about wanting to understand her world, her senses, her daily life. Prying is about extracting information. It is about filling a gap in your own knowledge, often with a sense of urgency or judgment.

You can tell the difference by your tone and your follow-up. If you ask, "Why did you leave?" and she gives a short answer, and you immediately ask, "But really, why?" that is prying. If you ask, "What was the first thing you bought when you got here?" and she talks about the shop, the price, the feeling of holding the item, that is curiosity.

Curiosity invites her into the moment; prying pulls her out of it.

## How do I start the conversation without a formal "interview" setup?

Do not sit down with a notebook and a pen. Do not say, "I want to record your life story." Instead, start with a shared activity; make a meal together; look at old photos; walk through a garden.

The conversation should emerge from the activity, not from a scheduled appointment. If you are cooking, ask about the recipe. If you are looking at photos, ask about the people in them. If you are walking, ask about the street names.

The context matters; a kitchen is a safe place. A living room with a camera on a tripod is not.

## What specific, low-pressure questions open the door to her story?

Use the "Sensory Anchor" technique. Instead of asking about the journey, ask about a specific object or sense.

- "I found your old recipe book. What was that spice you used in the stew?"
- "What did the light look like in this room in the photo?"
- "What song was on the radio when you first arrived?"
- "What was the first car in the family, and who was allowed to drive it?"

These questions are specific enough to picture; they ask about a thing, not a feeling; they can't be answered with yes or no. They bypass the trauma of the journey itself and focus on the culture she brought with her.

You are holding a photo of her childhood home. Instead of asking, "How did you feel when you left?" you ask, "What did the light look like in this room?" The answer is not a summary of grief. It is a description of the dust motes in the afternoon sun, the colour of the curtains, the smell of the floor. And from there, the story comes.

## How do I respond if she shuts down or changes the subject?

She might say, "It was hard, let's not talk about it." Or she might go quiet. Or she might change the subject to the weather.

Do not push; Do not ask, "But why?" Do not try to fix it; sit with the silence. In many cultures, silence is a form of processing or respect, not rejection.

If she shuts down, say, "Okay, I understand; I'm here if you ever want to talk." Then, change the subject to something light; this builds trust for future conversations. ## What people get wrong

**Asking for the "full story."** This is the most common mistake; it is too big; it is too heavy; it feels like a demand.

**Filling the silence.** When she pauses, do not rush to ask the next question. Let the silence be; it is part of the conversation.

**Correcting her.** If a date is wrong or a story has drifted, let it go. You are collecting how she remembers it, and that has its own truth.

**Waiting for a good time.** The good time rarely arrives, so a rough conversation made this month is better than a perfect one that never happens.

## How do I keep the conversation going if she gives short answers?

If she gives one-line answers, it is not because she is being difficult. It is because the question was too big.

Try a different angle. If she says, "It was hard," ask, "What was the hardest part of the first month?" If she says, "I don't remember," ask, "Do you remember the food?"

You can also look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) for practical follow-up techniques.

The goal is not to get a transcript; the goal is to connect. If you can do that, the rest will follow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it rude to ask my mum why she left her home country?

No, but it depends on the framing. Asking "Why" can feel like a demand for justification. Asking "What was it like?" invites a narrative; focus on the experience rather than the decision.

### What if my mum gets angry when I ask about the move?

Anger can be a mask for grief or shame. If she becomes angry, stop immediately, apologize for the discomfort, and change the subject. Do not push for an explanation.

### Should I record the conversation on my phone?

Only if she has given explicit permission, because the act of recording can make the conversation feel more formal and high-stakes. Start with just listening to ensure she feels comfortable sharing.

### How do I ask about her sacrifices without making her feel guilty?

Avoid the word "sacrifice." Instead, ask about her hopes and dreams at the time. This frames the story around her agency and future, not just her loss.

### What is the best way to ask my mum about her move to Australia?

The best way is to use sensory anchors, such as asking about a specific recipe or song, rather than requesting a full summary. This approach lowers the pressure and allows her to share details at her own pace, making the conversation feel like a shared memory rather than an interrogation.
