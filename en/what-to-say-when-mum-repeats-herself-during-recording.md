# What to say when mum repeats herself during recording?

Gentle scripts and technical reframing to handle repetition without correcting her, preserving the emotional weight of the moment.

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/8d513dd15eaabbdcfa7b.delivery-v1.webp>)

You do not need to correct them. Instead, gently validate the emotion behind the repetition with a phrase like, "I can see how much that meant to you," or simply pause and let the silence hold the moment. This acknowledges their feeling without challenging their memory, keeping the emotional flow intact for the recording.

You are sitting there with the microphone on, and she has just told you the same story for the third time. It feels frustrating, maybe even a little hurtful, as if she is not paying attention to you or the effort you are putting in. You might be wondering if you should stop the recording, or if this means the project is failing.

This is a very common moment for anyone recording a parent's life, especially when memory changes are part of the picture. It is easy to feel like you are the one who is failing because the conversation is not moving forward. But the truth is, the repetition is often the most important part of the recording.

You are not a journalist chasing a new fact. You are a witness to a life. When she repeats herself, she is not just recycling words; she is returning to a feeling that still matters to her. Your job in that moment is not to edit, but to connect.

## Why does mum repeat herself when she’s recording her story?

Repetition is rarely a sign of confusion or a lack of respect. For many people, especially those navigating early memory changes, repeating a story is a way of processing an emotion. It is a way of saying, *this still matters to me*.

Australian parents often deflect vulnerability with humor or practicality. For older generations, expressing deep emotion directly can feel unsafe. Repeating a story, particularly one about a love, a loss, or a significant achievement, can be a safe way to test if you are listening. It is a quiet way of asking, *do you still care?*

If you are unsure about the broader context of recording with early-stage dementia, it helps to understand that the goal is connection, not a perfect transcript. You can read more about [can I record my mother's voice if she has early-stage dementia](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) to see how this fits into the wider picture.

## What is the best script to use when she loops back to the same point?

The instinct is to say, "Mum, you've told me that before." This is the wrong move. It corrects her, which can feel like a rejection. Instead, you want to validate the emotion behind the repetition.

A simple, effective script is: **"I’ve heard this part before, and I love hearing it again because it tells me how important it was to you."**

This phrase does three things: it acknowledges that you are paying attention, it affirms the value of the story, and it reframes the repetition as a gift rather than a glitch. If you want to keep it even simpler, you can just pause, look at her, and say, "I can see how much that meant to you," then wait and let the silence do the work.

## How do I handle the guilt of knowing she’s said this before?

It is natural to feel a bit of guilt or frustration, thinking you should be getting more out of her time. But try to shift your perspective. In voice preservation, a repeated phrase often carries the highest emotional charge.

Think about it this way: if someone tells you a story once, you hear the facts, but if they tell it three times, you hear the feeling. The second and third times are often where the voice softens, where the breath changes, and where the true weight of the memory comes out. That is the data you are trying to capture; it is not noise, it is the signal.

## Should I stop the recording if the repetition becomes distressing for her?

If the repetition starts to cause her distress, or if she becomes agitated, then yes, stop. You are not a machine, and she is not a project. Her comfort is always more important than the recording.

If she seems tired or confused, you can gently close the session. Say something like, "We can talk about this another time. I just wanted to listen." You can try again later, or simply listen without recording. There is no prize for finishing a session, and no penalty for stopping early.

## What is the difference between a memory loop and a meaningful reflection?

A memory loop can sometimes feel like a stuck record, where the words are the same but the energy is flat. A meaningful reflection, on the other hand, feels alive. Even if the words are similar, there is a shift in tone, a pause, a sigh, or a smile.

You do not need to diagnose her; you just need to listen to the tone. If she is engaging with you, if there is a spark in her eyes or a warmth in her voice, it is likely a meaningful reflection. If she seems lost or distressed, it might be a loop. In either case, your response should be gentle and validating.

## How does repetition actually help the final audiobook or book?

In the final edit, repetition can be a powerful narrative device. Instead of cutting it out, it can be used to create a rhythmic or thematic motif. It highlights what is most important to her.

The repetition itself becomes the emotional anchor. The way she returns to the same phrase, with the same breath and the same weight, tells you what truly mattered. This is more valuable than the facts. The repetition becomes the heart of the story, not a flaw in it.

## What people get wrong

**Correcting the facts.** If she says she was twenty when she got married, but she was actually twenty-one, let it go. You are not writing a biography; you are capturing a memory. Correcting her breaks the flow and can make her feel foolish.

**Treating it as a burden.** If you sigh, or if your tone changes, she will feel it. Repetition is not a problem to be solved; it is a part of the human experience. Approach it with patience and curiosity.

**Focusing on the interview format.** If the conversation feels like a formal interview, it can exacerbate anxiety and lead to more repetition. Keep it loose and conversational. You can read more about [how to record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>) to see how to keep the tone natural.

**Ignoring the emotional weight.** The words are less important than the feeling, so if you focus only on getting the facts right, you will miss the point of the recording.

## A moment in the kitchen

You are sitting at the kitchen table, and your mum is telling you about her wedding. She has told you this story three times already. Instead of saying, "Mum, you've told me that," you say, "Tell me about the dress again. I want to know more about the fabric."

This redirects the focus to a sensory detail; it breaks the loop without confrontation. She will likely light up, describing the lace, the weight of it, the way it felt. You have moved the conversation forward, but you have also honoured the emotion behind the repetition.

## The next step

Start with a single, low-pressure session. You don’t need to plan a whole book; just record one conversation where you let her tell the story she wants to tell, even if she repeats it. This small step preserves the voice and the emotion, which is the most important part.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### ### Is it okay to edit out the repeated parts later?

Yes, but keep the most emotionally charged repetition. In the final product, the repetition can be a powerful narrative device, so don't cut everything. The tone and pace of the repetition often convey more emotion than the words themselves, so preserving that emotional anchor is valuable.

### ### What if she gets upset that I’m recording her?

Stop immediately; respect her boundaries. You can try again later, or simply listen without recording. Her comfort is more important than the recording. If she is distressed, the conversation is over for that moment, and you can revisit it when she is ready.

### ### How long should I let the repetition continue?

There is no fixed time, but watch for her energy. If she remains engaged and her voice stays warm, let it continue. If she becomes flat or agitated, gently redirect or pause. The goal is to capture the feeling, not to endure a long session.

### ### Can I use a prompt to help her move on?

Yes, but keep it gentle. Instead of "Let's move on," try "And what happened next?" or "How did that make you feel?" This keeps the flow forward without rejecting the previous part. It acknowledges the current story while opening a door to the next one, maintaining the emotional connection.

### ### Is this a sign that her dementia is getting worse?

Not necessarily. Repetition can be a coping mechanism or a way of processing memory. It is not a direct indicator of progression. Consult her doctor for medical advice, but don't let it stop you from recording. The repetition is often a sign of emotional significance, not cognitive decline.
