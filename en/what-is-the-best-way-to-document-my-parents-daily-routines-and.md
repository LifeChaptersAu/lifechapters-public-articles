# How Do I Document My Parent's Personality Before Aged Care?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/bb2b16b28499a07b182c.delivery-v1.webp>)

Ask about specific daily rituals and sensory comforts rather than general preferences, and record their voice if they are comfortable with it. This creates a practical profile of who they are that complements medical plans, helping caregivers understand not just what your parent needs, but who they are.

ACAT assessments, EPOAs, and Medicare details cover the legal and medical requirements, but they often miss the specific way a person takes their tea, their particular laugh, or the song that instantly calms them. The paperwork treats the parent as a set of needs to be met, rather than as an individual with a distinct daily rhythm.

There is a fear that sits underneath the logistics. It is the worry that once the door closes and the uniform appears, the person you know will fade into the background of a case number. The medical record will be accurate, but it will be incomplete. It will tell the nurses what to do, but not who they are doing it for.

This is for you if you are preparing for a move and want to bridge that gap. It is not about legal compliance, though that is handled elsewhere. It is about preserving the human texture of your parent’s life so that the new environment can hold space for it.

## Why do medical records fail to capture who your parent is?

Clinical assessments are designed to identify risks, dependencies, and medical histories. They are excellent at their job, but they are not designed to capture personality. A doctor will note that your parent has early-stage dementia, but they will not know that the smell of eucalyptus on a handkerchief brings them back to the present, or that they hate the sound of a specific radio station.

The *Aged Care Act 2024* emphasises person-centred care, but the implementation of that phrase relies entirely on staff knowing the individual. If the only information the staff have is the medical chart, the care will be competent but generic. It will be safe, but it may not feel like home.

The gap between "what the patient needs" and "who the person is" is where anxiety often lives. Your parent knows they are different; they know what comforts them. If that knowledge stays with you, it is lost the moment you leave the room.

## What specific "soft" data should you document before the move?

You do not need to write a biography; you need a profile of their daily existence. Think about the small, repetitive things that make up their week, such as whether they need the curtains drawn or open, or if they take their tea with exactly one sugar cube.

Sensory anchors are also important. Is there a specific music genre, a scent, or a texture that calms them? Conversely, are there noises or lights that agitate them? Social preferences matter too. Do they want visitors, or do they prefer a quiet room? Do they have a specific way of greeting people that staff should mimic to put them at ease?

Humour and quirks are the final piece. What are their specific jokes? How do they like their socks folded? These details seem trivial, but they are the proof of their identity. When you are gathering these details, it helps to look at how you might preserve the broader life story that led to these habits. You can find more on [how to preserve your parent's life story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-practical-steps-should-i-take-to-preserve-my-parents-life-story>), which covers the deeper context that often explains why certain rituals matter so much.

## How do you ask questions without it feeling like an interrogation?

Your parent may be resistant to feeling "assessed" by their own family. If you sit them down with a notebook and a list, it can feel like a job interview. The key is to make it conversational. Sit with them, have a cup of tea, and ask about their week rather than their "preferences."

You might ask, "What was the best part of your day yesterday?" or "Do you remember what you had for breakfast this morning? Did you like it?" Let them talk; listen for the things they mention offhand. If they complain about the light, that is a data point. If they mention a song they heard on the radio, that is another. You are not collecting answers; you are observing their life. For more on how to keep the conversation natural and flowing, see [how to get a parent to talk about their life](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-to-get-a-parent-to-talk-about-their-life>).

## What is the role of audio recording in preserving their voice?

A written note is good, but a voice is better, because hearing your parent say "I like the way the light comes in at four o'clock" carries a weight that text does not, capturing their tone, hesitation, and warmth. If your parent has early-stage dementia, this is particularly important. Dementia Australia guidance suggests that maintaining routine and familiarity is key to reducing distress. Recording their voice allows you to share their actual words with the care staff. It is not about testing their memory; it is about capturing their comfort.

If you are unsure about the ethics or the practicalities of recording a parent with cognitive decline, it is worth reading [can I record my mother's voice if she has early-stage dementia](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) to understand the nuances of consent and comfort.

## How do you share this information with the care facility?

Create a one-page "Personality Profile" rather than a lengthy document, as brevity aids staff retention in a busy environment where they manage many residents simultaneously. Keep it simple. You might list their likes, such as music, food, or topics of conversation, and their dislikes, such as noises, smells, or certain times of day.

Include their comforts, such as specific objects, routines, or phrases that help, and their triggers, which are things that cause agitation or anxiety. Print this out. Give a copy to the head nurse and a copy to the day manager. When you visit, mention it casually. "By the way, I mentioned they love the sound of the piano; maybe that could be played in the afternoon?"

## What people get wrong

**Focusing on the past.** While history is important, the care staff need to know about the *present*. How does your parent behave today? What do they need now? A detailed history of their youth is less useful than a note that they need their reading glasses by the bed at 7 AM.

**Assuming they will forget.** Even if your parent has dementia, they remember their preferences. They remember that you like their tea strong; they remember that the new environment feels wrong. Your documentation helps them feel understood, not just managed.

**Making it a one-time event.** People change and preferences shift, so if you notice your parent has started to dislike a certain food, update the profile to reflect this living document rather than treating it as a museum piece.

**Ignoring the staff's perspective.** They are human too, so if you give them a clear, concise, and respectful guide to your parent, they will be more likely to use it, whereas a rambling, emotional, or overly detailed narrative may leave them unsure where to start.

## A quiet afternoon in the kitchen

In one observed conversation on a Tuesday afternoon, the light was coming through the window at a low angle. Your parent was sitting at the table, holding a cup of tea. They were quiet.

You sat down; you did not pull out a list; you just sat.

They said, "It's a bit cold in here today."

You said, "Is it? I didn't notice."

They said, "It is. I like the window open."

You wrote that down, noting that they like the window open because they feel cold when it is closed.

They took a sip of tea and said, "This is good. Not too sweet."

You wrote that down, noting that they prefer their tea not too sweet.

It is small; it is not a life story; but it is a piece of them. And when the nurse in the new facility sees that note, they will know to open the window. They will know to make the tea less sweet. And your parent will feel, for a moment, that they are not a case number. They are someone who likes the window open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much detail is too much for a care facility?

Share what helps the staff understand the person in the present moment. Focus on current preferences, sensory triggers, and daily routines rather than exhaustive biographical history. A concise one-page profile is often more useful than a long document, as it is easier for busy staff to reference quickly during care.

### Can I record my parent’s voice if they have dementia?

Yes, if they are comfortable with it. Focus on capturing their reactions and preferences in the present moment rather than testing their factual recall. Ensure you have their consent if they are capable of giving it, and check with their healthcare team if capacity is a concern. The goal is comfort and connection, not assessment.

### What if my parent refuses to talk about their past?

Respect their boundary and do not push. Shift the focus to their current preferences, daily routines, and immediate comfort needs. You can still document valuable "soft data" by observing their behaviour, noting what they like and dislike, and asking simple questions about their present experience rather than their history.

### Is there a legal requirement to provide a personality profile?

No, it is not a legal document like an Enduring Power of Attorney. However, it is strongly recommended for person-centred care. Providing this information helps the care facility meet their obligations to provide individualised, respectful care, and it ensures that your parent’s unique needs and preferences are understood beyond the clinical basics.

### How do I handle sibling disagreements about what to share?

Focus on your parent’s best interests and current well-being. Prioritise information that directly impacts their comfort and safety, such as medical preferences or sensory triggers. Try to keep the discussion separate from family dynamics, and if possible, agree on a shared document that reflects the parent’s own stated wishes and observed needs.
