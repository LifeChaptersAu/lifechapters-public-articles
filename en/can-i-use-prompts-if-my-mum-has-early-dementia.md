# Can I Use Prompts If My Mum Has Early Dementia?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/1b6af1ee60e680026cbe.delivery-v1.webp?hero=9439a023da40e2cc>)

Yes, you can use prompts, but they must be simple, sensory, and open-ended. Avoid complex chronological questions that require working memory. Instead, use single-sense triggers like a photo or a smell, and yes/no or two-choice questions to reduce cognitive load. This allows your mother to access long-term memory and emotional truth without the stress of constructing a narrative, ensuring the recording captures her genuine voice.

You are sitting across from your mum, and you want to ask her about her life. You are worried that if you ask the wrong thing, she will get confused, or frustrated, or shut down. That fear is common, and it is understandable. You do not want to be the person who makes the conversation difficult.

You do not need to be a therapist or a professional interviewer to have a meaningful conversation. You just need to change the shape of your questions. Instead of asking for a story, ask for a detail. Instead of asking for a timeline, ask for a feeling.

This is about the practical, gentle art of asking questions that work with her current abilities, not against them. If this feels like a lot, or if you want help structuring the final piece, there are options to support you, but the first step is simply changing how you ask.

## Why do complex questions shut down early-stage dementia?

When we ask "Tell me about your wedding day," we are asking for a complex cognitive task. The person has to retrieve the event, sequence the events within it, identify the people involved, and construct a narrative flow. For someone with early dementia, this working memory load is often too high.

The brain doesn't fail because the memory is gone. It fails because the architecture needed to build the sentence is missing. This is why a simple "Did you wear the blue dress or the white one?" works so well; it bypasses the need for narrative construction. It offers two clear paths, and the person can choose one. This is a standard communication strategy for dementia, but it is particularly useful for capturing life stories because it engages long-term memory rather than short-term processing.

## How do I structure a "sensory bridge" prompt?

A sensory bridge is a physical object or a specific sense that acts as a key to a memory. Instead of asking about a time, ask about a thing.

Hold a photograph in front of her. Do not ask "Who is this?" Ask "What does this house smell like?" Or if you have a recipe card, ask "Did you use the wooden spoon or the metal one for this?"

In Australia, many parents from the mid-20th century have strong sensory memories of specific locations. The sharp, yeasty scent of warm bread from the bakery on the corner, or the rhythmic clatter of the train passing the old house in Perth. These are not just facts; they are emotional anchors. Using these as prompts allows her to access the feeling of the moment without having to explain the logic of why it happened.

## What do I do when she gives a one-word answer?

If she says "Yes" or "No," or just a single word like "Blue," it can feel like the conversation has died, but it has just paused. Do not say "Oh, that's all?" or "Tell me more," because these phrases put pressure on her to perform. Instead, wait and let the silence sit for a few seconds, as often they will fill it.

If she doesn't, ask a very small follow-up, such as "Was it a light blue or a dark blue?" This keeps the cognitive load low. If you find yourself stuck in a loop of short answers, it might help to look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) for more specific techniques that avoid the pressure of a "correct" response.

## How do I keep the conversation flowing without it feeling like an interview?

An interview has a structure: question, answer, question, answer. A conversation has a flow; the difference is subtle but important.

In an interview, you are the driver; in a conversation, you are the passenger; let her steer. If she mentions a neighbour, let it sit there. Don't rush to the next question on your list. If she says "Nan was there," you can ask "What did Nan do when she was there?" rather than "Who else was at the wedding?"

This approach respects her autonomy. It signals that you are interested in her experience, not just her data. If you are struggling to keep the thread without it feeling like a cross-examination, [what to say when dad answers in three words](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-say-when-dad-answers-in-three-words>) offers a similar framework for keeping the tone light and the pressure low.

## When should I stop recording and just listen?

This is the most important part; you are not running a data collection exercise; you are spending time with your mother.

If she starts to look tired, or if she is repeating herself, or if she is getting agitated, stop. Turn off the recorder; just listen; you do not need to capture every word; you need to capture the connection.

Sometimes the most valuable part of the session is the quiet moment where she looks at the photo and smiles, and you both remember together. That moment is worth more than a perfect recording. If the conversation becomes emotional, it is okay to pause. ## What people get wrong

**Correcting them.** If she says "I was in London" but she was in Sydney, do not correct her. The location is less important than the feeling of the memory. Ask "What did the weather feel like?" to stay in the emotional truth.

**Asking for the whole story.** "Tell me about your childhood" is too big; "What was your favourite toy?" is a manageable piece.

**Rushing the session.** Try to keep segments to five minutes; this prevents fatigue and frustration; if she is tired, stop; you can do it again tomorrow.

**Treating it as a test.** If she gets a detail wrong, it is not a failure, but rather a memory that should be allowed to be.

## What is the next step?

You need one object; a photo, a letter, a piece of clothing.

Sit down with your mum; hold the object; ask one simple, two-choice question. "Did you like this colour, or did you prefer the other one?" Record the answer, even if it is just a few words.

This small act captures a genuine moment of connection. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it better to use a voice recorder or a smartphone?

A smartphone is fine, but ensure you are in a quiet room and hold the device close to capture clarity, as hearing loss is common in early dementia. The goal is not professional audio, but a clear enough recording that you can understand what she said.

### Should I tell her we are recording for a book?

Yes, transparency builds trust; Explain it simply: "I want to keep your voice so we can hear it again later." Avoid complex details about publishing or editing; keep the purpose simple and personal.

### What if she repeats the same dementia memory?

This is a sign of comfort, not failure; let her repeat it. The nuances in tone and emotion are part of the legacy. You are not collecting new data; you are capturing her voice and her feelings.

### Can I use prompts if she is in the middle stage?

Yes, but simplify further. Use yes/no questions and focus on immediate feelings rather than past events. The goal is to engage her present experience, not to retrieve distant memories.

### How long should a recording session be?

Keep it to five to ten minutes. Watch for signs of fatigue, such as yawning or looking away, and stop before they appear. A short, positive session is better than a long, tiring one.
