# What To Do If My Pop Is Too Shy To Talk?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/b0110eab333e684c23aa.delivery-v1.webp?hero=e5f1e5b1b58e8683>)

Stop asking direct questions like "Tell me about your childhood" and start creating low-pressure environments where your father can share indirectly. Use parallel activities like fixing a car, looking at old photos, or cooking together to lower the intensity of eye contact. Let him talk about objects or tasks, and listen for the stories that emerge naturally.

You sit down with your dad on the cold concrete slab of the back porch, maybe with a footy game playing on the radio in the background, and you ask him to tell you about his life. He looks at you, shifts in his seat, and says something like "there's not much to tell" or "I was just a normal bloke." The conversation stalls, and you feel like you've failed.

You are not failing, but you are just using the wrong tool for the job. When you ask a reserved Australian man to perform his life story on demand, you are asking him to do something that goes against his grain. It is not that he doesn't have stories; it is that the format of "interview" feels like a test he is about to fail. The fix is not to try harder to get him to talk, but to change the environment so that talking becomes the easy path, not the hard one.

## Why does my dad shut down when I ask about his life?

Direct eye contact and open-ended questions create a high-pressure situation. For many men, especially those of a certain generation, this triggers a defensive response because they feel exposed, as if they are on stage. The "tall poppy" mentality and the cultural expectation that men should be stoic and self-reliant often mean that vulnerability is guarded closely.

When you ask "How was your childhood?", you are asking for a summary. Summaries require judgment, [so he has](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) to decide what is important, what is embarrassing, and what is worth sharing. That is a lot of cognitive load, and if the answer is "I don't know" or "It was fine," the conversation dies. The goal is to remove the need for a summary and move from "tell me about your life" to "look at this."

## How do I start a conversation without it feeling like an interview?

The key is to shift from face-to-face to side-by-side. Australian men often communicate better when they are not making direct eye contact, which aligns with general Australian cultural norms for male bonding where shared activity is a safer space for conversation than direct interrogation. For instance, leaning on a fence post while looking out at the paddock, or working together on a carburetor, creates a natural barrier to direct scrutiny that allows words to flow more easily.

- Mowing the lawn together.
- Working on a shed project or fixing a car.

In these settings, you are not staring at each other, but doing something else, which lowers the intensity. You can ask questions while your hands are busy, and he can answer while his hands are busy, so it feels like a chat, not an interview. ## What are the best "low-pressure" activities to trigger memories?

Objects are powerful triggers. Instead of asking "What was your job like?", place a specific object in front of him, such as a rusted spanner, a photo, a ticket stub, or a piece of machinery. Ask, "Do you remember this?"

This shifts the cognitive load because he doesn't have to generate a narrative from scratch; he just has to recall a specific memory associated with the object. The story often comes out of that single detail. For example, if you show him an old wrench, he might talk about the first car he ever fixed, or if you show him a photo of his father, he might talk about his grandfather's hands. The object is the anchor.

## How do I handle the silence when he gives short answers?

This is the hardest part, as he will likely give you short answers such as "It was hard," "I don't know," or "Yeah." In these moments, it is crucial not to push or say "But what happened?" or "Tell me more," because that feels like pressure. Instead, use the "3-Word Rule." If he answers in three words, acknowledge it and let the silence sit. Say, "Yeah, it was a tough time," then stop talking and wait.

Often, the silence is uncomfortable for both of you, but it is in that silence that the next story often emerges. He might say, "Yeah, it was. We didn't have much money," and then he might go on to tell you about the specific ways they managed. If you are struggling with this, you can read [what to say when dad answers in three words](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-say-when-dad-answers-in-three-words>).

## What questions should I ask my dad about his childhood?

Avoid broad questions and ask about specific, sensory details instead.

- What did the kitchen smell like?
- What was the first car in the family?
- Who was the loudest person at a family gathering?
- What did you do with your first pay packet?

These questions are easy to answer because they are specific. They don't require a summary, but a memory.

## What people get wrong

**Asking for a summary.** "Tell me about your life" is too big; it asks for a judgment; instead, ask for a moment.

**Pushing through silence.** If he gives a short answer, pushing for more feels like an interrogation. Let the silence sit; the story often comes after.

**Doing it in one sitting.** An hour is plenty; two is too many; tiredness flattens a story. A short, frequent chat is better than a long, exhausting session.

**Correcting him.** If a date is wrong or a story has drifted, let it go. You are collecting how he remembers it, and that has its own truth.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for my dad to change the subject when I ask about his past?

Yes, this is a common defense mechanism for emotionally reserved men. It signals that the topic feels too vulnerable or that he doesn't know how to articulate it. Rather than forcing the topic, simply acknowledge the shift and wait for a natural opening to return to the subject later, perhaps through a related object or activity.

### What if my dad says he has nothing interesting to say?

This is often a humility or self-deprecation response, not a fact. Validate his feeling by saying "I know it feels that way," but gently counter with a specific detail you know, such as "But I remember you telling me about the flood in '72." This provides a concrete anchor for the conversation.

### Can I record him without him knowing?

No, always ask for permission. However, you can frame it as "I'm just keeping a record for the family" rather than "I'm making a book," which lowers the stakes. This makes the act of recording feel less like a performance and more like a simple act of preservation.

### How long should these conversations be?

Keep them short, 15–20 minutes; introverted individuals need time to recharge. A short, frequent chat is better than a long, exhausting session. This allows the conversation to end on a positive note, making it more likely he will agree to another one.

### What if he only talks about work and never about his feelings?

That is his comfort zone; start there. Work stories often contain hidden emotional narratives about his values, fears, and relationships. Let the emotional layer emerge naturally, rather than forcing it. This respects his boundaries while still capturing the depth of his experience.
