# How Do I Structure A Family Biography Interview?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/4d0faec63e4486aff00e.delivery-v1.webp?hero=0177976cb4a89432>)

Structure the conversation around three natural life phases: their childhood and roots, their adult journey and turning points, and their reflections on what matters now. This narrative arc allows the story to flow emotionally rather than chronologically, turning a "job interview" into a shared memory session where the emotional core emerges naturally without you needing to be a professional writer.

You have probably sat down with a parent, the smell of their kitchen coffee still in the air, ready to capture their life, and felt the air go tight. You ask a question, they give a short answer, and you both sit in a silence that feels heavy. It is easy to feel like you are conducting a performance review rather than sharing a memory.

The fear of being an interrogator is often the biggest barrier to starting. But the structure of the conversation is what changes the dynamic. If you move away from a list of facts and toward a narrative arc, the conversation relaxes.

It is not about being a professional writer. It is about creating a space where the story can flow.

## Why does interviewing my parent feel like a job interview?

The feeling comes from the shape of the questions. When you ask, "What year did you marry?" or "Where did you go to school?" you are asking for data. Your parent hears a request for a fact, so they give you a fact, and the conversation ends there.

It feels like a job interview because you are the one asking, and they are the one answering. There is no shared space; you are extracting information, and they are providing it. This dynamic is exhausting for both of you.

The fix is to change the goal. You are not collecting data; you are listening to a story. When you ask, "Tell me about the day you met your partner," you are inviting them into a memory. They have to paint the picture, and you get to see it. That is a conversation, not an interrogation.

If you are struggling with this specific anxiety, it helps to look at [how to record your dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>). The core issue is rarely the technology or the setting; it is the intent behind the question.

## What is the three-act structure for a family life story?

Think of a life story like a movie. [It has a](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) beginning, a middle, and an end. For a biography interview, this translates into three phases: Roots, Journey, and Legacy.

**Roots** is about where they came from; this is the sensory world of their childhood. What did the house smell like? Who was in the family? What games did they play? This phase is usually the easiest to start with because it is safe and nostalgic.

**Journey** is the middle of the life, where the work, the love, the moving, and the challenges live. It is the active part of the story, testing the character through hard decisions and big changes.

**Legacy** is the reflection; this is about what matters now. What do they want the next generation to know? What are the values they hold most dear? This phase is often the most powerful, because it is the parent speaking directly to their future.

You do not need to be rigid. If they start talking about their wedding in the Roots phase, let them, because the structure is a map, not a fence.

## How do I start the conversation without asking for data?

Start with the senses; avoid dates and titles. Instead, ask about the physical world of their early life.

Try questions like:

- What did your childhood home smell like?
- What was the sound of your street in the morning?
- Who was the person you felt closest to when you were ten?

These questions cannot be answered with a single word. They require a description, and description is where the story lives.

In Australia, we have shared historical touchstones that can act as anchors. For some, it might be major national events; for others, it might be a local event. These events are shared memories, and they can open a door that a personal question might not.

## What are the key questions for the "Journey" phase?

The Journey phase is where the life becomes complex. Here, you want to ask about decisions and turning points.

- What was the hardest decision you had to make in your twenties?
- Tell me about the time you moved to a new city. What did you leave behind?
- What was the most surprising thing that happened to you in your career?

Notice the difference; "What was your job?" is data; "What was the most surprising thing?" is a story. It invites them to reflect on their experience, not just their resume.

This is also where you might hit a wall. If they give short answers, it is not because they are withholding. It is often because the question was too small. ## How do I handle the silence when they stop talking?

Silence is not a failure; it is a pause. When your parent stops talking, do not rush to fill the gap. Take a breath; let the silence sit for a few seconds.

Often, the silence is because they are thinking. Or because they are feeling something they haven't said yet. If you jump in with the next question, you interrupt that processing.

If they are truly stuck, you can offer a gentle prompt. "Is there a part of that you remember more clearly?" or "How did that make you feel at the time?" These questions invite them back into the memory without forcing a specific answer.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it as a one-off event.** A life story is not a single interview; it is a series of conversations; one hour is plenty; two is too many; tiredness flattens a story.

**Correcting the facts.** If they say they were born in 1950 and the record says 1952, let it go. You are recording their memory, not their biography; their memory has its own truth.

**Forcing chronology.** If they jump from their wedding to their retirement, let them. The emotional flow is more important than the timeline. A story that flows is a story that gets remembered.

**Focusing on the recording.** The phone should be a passive observer, so place it on a stand facing them but not in their face, because if you are watching the levels, you are not listening to the story.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a family biography interview be?

Aim for 60 to 90 minutes. Any longer and both you and your parent may feel fatigued, which can lead to shorter, less detailed answers. It is better to have several shorter conversations than one long, exhausting session.

### Should I send questions in advance?

Only if your parent is comfortable with it. For many, seeing a list of questions beforehand triggers the "exam" feeling. It is often better to have a loose mental map and let the conversation flow naturally during the session.

### What if my parent gets emotional?

Pause; do not rush to fix it. Acknowledge the emotion by saying something like "That sounds like a heavy time" and let them lead. If they need a break, take one. It is okay to stop and resume another day.

### Do I need to ask about their childhood if they don't want to?

No; respect their boundaries. You can start with the "Journey" phase, which covers their adult life, if that is where they feel comfortable. The story can still be complete and meaningful without the childhood details.

### How do I structure a family biography interview if they jump around?

Let them jump. The three-act structure is a guide, not a strict timeline. If they move from their wedding to their retirement, follow their emotional flow. You can gently steer them back to a phase later if needed, but forcing chronology often kills the natural storytelling.
