# What Do I Do If Siblings Disagree On What To Share With Aged Care?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/a3cabed765f8beb922b7.delivery-v1.webp?hero=97e48d69ecebcf48>)

Create a neutral Care Context Summary that lists only the factual, functional details the facility needs for daily care, such as medications, routines, and dietary restrictions. Explicitly exclude subjective family history or private anecdotes that are currently in dispute. This allows you to meet the facility's operational requirements without forcing a family debate over private narratives.

You are sitting at the desk, trying to fill out the intake forms for your parent's move into aged care, and you have hit a wall. Your sibling has drawn a line in the sand about what can be shared, and now you are stuck between the facility's requirements and their boundaries. It feels like you are being forced to choose between doing the right thing for your parent and keeping the peace with your brother or sister.

This is not just a logistical headache; it is an emotional one. You worry that withholding information might harm your parent's care, but you also know that forcing a narrative can damage a relationship that is already strained. You do not need to resolve the entire family history to get your parent into care. You need a way to separate the clinical necessities from the private stories.

The solution is not a legal battle or a family therapy session. It is a document. A simple, neutral summary that tells the carers what they need to know to keep your parent safe and comfortable, while leaving the disputed parts out of the picture.

## Why does the aged care facility need a life story for intake?

Facilities ask for background information because they are required to provide person-centred care under the Australian Aged Care Act 2019. This means they need to understand your parent's preferences, cultural background, and daily habits to tailor their care plan. However, this is not a request for a memoir. It is a request for context that helps the carers understand how to interact with your parent effectively.

For example, knowing that your parent was a teacher might help a carer understand their patience for routine, or knowing their religious practices might inform their dietary needs. The facility is not interested in the drama of your family dynamics; they are interested in the practical details that make daily life easier and more dignified for your parent. If you find yourself struggling to organise these details into a coherent narrative that the facility can use, you might find it helpful to look at [how to turn scattered stories into a coherent narrative](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-can-i-turn-my-parents-scattered-stories-into-a-coherent-narrative>), which offers a framework for structuring information without the emotional weight of a full biography.

## What is the difference between clinical information and personal history?

Clinical information is factual, functional, and directly related to safety and comfort. This includes medication lists, allergies, mobility issues, dietary restrictions, and preferred routines. Personal history is subjective, narrative, and often emotional. This includes childhood memories, family disputes, and private anecdotes.

The key distinction is that clinical information is necessary for care, while personal history is optional for care. A carer needs to know that your parent has a peanut allergy, but they do not need to know the story of why your family stopped eating peanuts together. By keeping these two categories separate, you can provide the facility with everything they need to do their job well, without having to navigate the minefield of family secrets.

## How do I explain to my sibling that I am not sharing their private details?

This conversation is often the hardest part. You do not need to convince your sibling that their perspective is wrong; you just need to clarify what you are doing and why. You can say something like, "I am filling out the care forms with the factual details the facility needs, such as medications and routines. I am not including the personal stories we disagree on, because that is not part of the clinical intake."

This approach removes the element of betrayal. You are not sharing their secrets; you are simply not sharing them. You are also not ignoring the facility's needs; you are meeting them with the most relevant information. If your sibling is still upset, you can remind them that the goal is to get your parent into care smoothly, and that a neutral document is the least confrontational way to do that.

## What should be included in a neutral Care Context Summary?

A Care Context Summary is a one-page document that lists the essential facts about your parent's daily life. It should include:

- **Medical facts:** Medications, allergies, and any conditions that affect daily care.
- **Routines:** Preferred wake-up and sleep times, bathing preferences, and mobility needs.
- **Dietary needs:** Restrictions, preferences, and any cultural or religious dietary requirements.
- **Key contacts:** Who to call in an emergency, and who is the primary decision-maker.
- **Communication style:** How your parent prefers to be spoken to, and any signs of distress or discomfort.

This document is not a judgment on your family's history. It is a practical tool that helps the carers understand your parent as a person, not just a patient. If you want to expand on the daily routines section, you can explore [the most effective way to document daily routines](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-is-the-best-way-to-document-my-parents-daily-routines-and>) for more detailed guidance on capturing these habits accurately.

## How do I handle a sibling who refuses to provide any information?

If your sibling is withholding information that is critical to your parent's safety, such as a severe allergy or a mobility issue, you need to address this directly. You can say, "I understand you have concerns about sharing personal history, but I need to ensure the facility knows about \[specific medical issue\] to keep Mum safe."

If your sibling is refusing to share information because of a past dispute, you can use the "Two-Envelope" Method. Envelope A contains the clinical and functional details that are undisputed and necessary for care. Envelope B contains the personal and contextual details that are disputed or private. You provide Envelope A to the facility and keep Envelope B for the family. This allows you to meet the facility's needs without forcing a debate over the contents of Envelope B. If you are still stuck, you might find it helpful to look at [general questions about family dynamics](<https://lifechapters.com.au/FAQ>) to see how other families have navigated similar situations.

## What people get wrong

**Trying to win the argument.** The goal is not to prove your sibling wrong; it is to get your parent into care. Arguing over the narrative distracts from the practical task at hand.

**Withholding critical safety information.** If your sibling is refusing to share an allergy or a mobility issue, you must share it. This is not a matter of opinion; it is a matter of safety.

**Assuming the facility needs a memoir.** They do not need a memoir; they need a care plan. Clarifying this distinction can defuse a lot of tension.

**Waiting for a family meeting.** You do not need to resolve the family dispute to fill out the intake forms. You can do this solo, using the Care Context Summary as your guide.

## When should I involve a professional to mediate this disagreement?

If the disagreement is preventing your parent from getting the care they need, or if it is causing significant distress for you or your sibling, it may be time to involve a professional. This could be a family mediator, a social worker, or a lawyer. A professional can help you navigate the legal and emotional complexities of the situation and ensure that your parent's best interests are protected.

If you are the primary liaison and feel stuck between the facility’s needs and your sibling’s boundaries, you can start by drafting a simple one-page "Care Context Summary" focusing only on daily routines and medical facts. This low-effort step often resolves the immediate intake pressure without requiring a family meeting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need my sibling's legal consent to share my parent's medical history?

Legal requirements vary by jurisdiction and depend on your specific authority documents. You should consult a legal professional to review your Enduring Power of Attorney or guardianship documents, as these determine your right to access and share health information without your sibling's agreement.

### Can I share my parent's story with the facility if my sibling objects?

You can share factual care information, but sharing subjective personal history that is disputed may escalate family conflict. It is best to stick to the clinical and functional details that are necessary for care, and leave the personal narratives for a later, more appropriate time.

### What if the facility insists on a full biography?

Most facilities do not require a full biography; they require a care plan. Clarify that you are providing a "Care Context Summary" rather than a memoir, and explain that this document contains the practical details they need to tailor care to your parent's preferences.

### How do I stop my sibling from controlling what I say to the carers?

You cannot control their speech, but you can set the boundary of what you will share, focusing on verified facts and the parent's expressed wishes. You are responsible for providing the facility with the information they need to care for your parent, and you are not obligated to share every detail your sibling wants you to include.

### Is it wrong to withhold family secrets from the aged care team?

It is not wrong to protect privacy, but it is important to share any information that affects the parent's safety, dignity, or emotional well-being in care. If a secret is relevant to how the carers should interact with your parent, it should be shared. If it is not relevant to their daily care, it can be kept private.
