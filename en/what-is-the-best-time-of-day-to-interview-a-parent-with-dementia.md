# What Is The Best Time Of Day To Interview A Parent With Dementia?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/bb2b16b28499a07b182c.delivery-v1.webp?hero=f4ad96d543703c40>)

The best time of day to interview a parent with dementia is usually late morning, between 9:30 and 11:30 AM, when they are often most alert and settled. However, every person is different, so you should observe their specific rhythm over a few days to find their personal peak alertness before attempting any structured memory capture.

You have probably tried to sit down and talk, only to find the conversation stalling, or worse, turning into a moment of confusion that leaves you both feeling drained. It is a painful experience, not because you are doing something wrong, but because the timing is likely off. You are trying to capture a life story, but the brain is not in a state to retrieve it.

This is not a failure of your patience or your questions. It is a matter of biological rhythm. The brain has peaks and troughs, and for someone living with dementia, those troughs can be particularly sharp. The goal is not to force a conversation, but to find the specific time of day when their mind is most open and their energy is highest.

We are going to look at how to find that window for your specific parent, because it is rarely the same for everyone.

## Why is the "morning golden window" not always the best time for your parent?

General advice often points to the morning as the best time, and for many, this is true. After a night's rest, the brain has had a chance to reset, leading to higher energy and focus in the early hours. Dementia Australia notes that some people living with dementia may be more active in the morning or around midday, and that regular visits, even if they’re brief, can make a real difference.

However, "morning" is a broad category. For some, the peak is at 8 AM, right after waking. For others, it is not until 10:30 AM, once they have had their breakfast and their daily medications have reached peak effect. Timing may need to align with when daily medications, such as those for blood pressure or cognition, have reached their strongest point, which is often late morning. It is best to consult with a geriatrician or pharmacist for general advice on how medication timing might affect alertness, rather than giving specific medical advice.

If you assume the morning is automatically best, you might miss the specific hour that works for your parent. The "golden window" is personal, not universal.

## How do I identify my parent’s specific "window of clarity" in the first week?

The best way to find this time is to observe, not to interview. For the first few days, resist the urge to ask deep questions. Instead, watch for the moments when your parent is most engaged, least agitated, and most willing to engage in simple conversation.

You might notice that at 9 AM, they are still groggy and confused about the date. By 10:30 AM, they are chatting about the weather or a recent meal with clarity. By 2 PM, they might be restless or repeating themselves. That 10:30 AM slot is your window.

This observation period is crucial because it removes the pressure of performance. You are not testing their memory; you are simply mapping their energy. Once you have identified this time, you can schedule your actual conversations there.

## What are the early signs that my parent is entering the "sundowning" phase?

Sundowning is a term used to describe the late afternoon and evening increase in confusion, restlessness, and agitation. It is not experienced by everyone, but for those who do, the evening can be particularly difficult.

If you notice your parent becoming more confused, asking the same questions repeatedly, or showing signs of distress as the day progresses, this is likely sundowning. It is a sign that the brain is struggling to process the day's events, and it is not the right time for memory capture.

If you see agitation or pacing, it is likely sundowning or distress, and you should stop the conversation immediately. Pushing through this phase can cause lasting negative associations with talking about the past.

## How long should a memory capture session be to avoid cognitive overload?

A session of 15 to 20 minutes is often ideal because shorter durations are better for maintaining focus. This is long enough to get into a rhythm and share a few meaningful details, but short enough to avoid the fatigue that sets in when the brain is working hard to retrieve memories.

If you find that the conversation is flowing well, it is tempting to keep going. But for someone with dementia, the effort of retrieval is exhausting. Ending the session while it is still going well leaves a positive impression, and they are more likely to be open to another conversation the next day.

## What should I do if my parent becomes confused or distressed during the session?

If your parent becomes confused or distressed, it is a signal that their brain is no longer in a state to engage in that type of conversation, so it is important to pause the interaction rather than trying to steer them back to the topic or correct them.

You can gently change the subject to something present and simple, such as asking about a photo on the table or offering a drink. If the distress continues, it is best to end the session and try again at a different time, or on a different day.

## A real example: The 4 PM failure and the 10 AM success

Sarah, a daughter from Melbourne, tried to interview her father at 4 PM. He became agitated, repeated the same story about his first job three times, and then shut down. She felt frustrated and worried she had lost the chance to hear his story.

The next morning, she waited until 10 AM; she sat with him, and he was calm. When she asked about his first job, he recalled specific details: the name of the boss, the smell of the workshop, and the feeling of pride when he got his first paycheck. The story was there all along; it just needed the right time to be accessed.

## What people get wrong

**Assuming one size fits all.** Relying on generic advice about "mornings" without observing your parent's specific rhythm can lead to missed opportunities.

**Pushing through distress.** Trying to get a full story out of someone who is clearly struggling is counterproductive, as it can cause lasting anxiety about conversation.

**Making it feel like an interview.** The tone should be conversational and low-pressure, because if it feels like a test, they will perform, and the performance will be flat. You might find it helpful to read [how to record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>).

**Ignoring medication timing.** If your parent takes medication that affects their alertness, the timing of the conversation should align with when that medication is most effective.

## The next step

Start by observing your parent’s daily rhythm for three days without attempting any recording. Note when they are most alert, least agitated, and most willing to engage. Use this observation to schedule a short, 15-minute "test" conversation during that window, focusing on a single, low-stakes memory.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I record my parent if they are in the late stages of dementia?

While complex narratives may be difficult, capturing short phrases, names, or reactions can still be meaningful; however, the "window of clarity" for structured interviews is typically lost in late stages. It is best to consult with a dementia specialist for stage-specific capabilities.

### Is it better to interview them in the morning or afternoon if they take medication?

It is generally best to wait until the medication has had time to work (usually 30–60 minutes post-dose), which often aligns with late morning, but you must consult their doctor for specific timing advice.

### What if my parent is a night owl and is more alert in the evening?

If your parent’s natural circadian rhythm is shifted, their "window of clarity" may be in the evening; however, be cautious of sundowning symptoms, which can mimic alertness but are often accompanied by agitation.

### How do I know if my parent is confused or just tired?

Confusion often involves disorientation to time/place or repetitive questioning, whereas tiredness involves slowed responses and eye-rubbing; if you see agitation or pacing, it is likely sundowning or distress, and you should stop.

### Should I bring other family members to the interview session?

It is usually best to keep the session one-on-one to reduce cognitive load and distraction; group dynamics can increase anxiety and make it harder for the person with dementia to focus on the conversation.
