# How Do I Get My Nan To Talk About Her Childhood?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/276f9f9adadfe913a722.delivery-v1.webp>)

You sit down with a cup of tea, ready to ask the questions you’ve been holding onto for years, and she stirs her tea without looking up. You ask about her school days, and she gives you a two-sentence summary that could have been written by anyone. It feels like a wall, not a conversation.

This is a common frustration for people in their fifties who are worried about losing these stories. The barrier is not that you are asking the wrong questions, but that the questions are too big and too formal. The goal is not to extract data, but to lower the pressure so the memories can flow naturally.

You know that pushing for a "life story" often results in silence, but you also know that the right nudge can open up hours of vivid detail. This is about creating a safe space for memory, not conducting an interview.

## Why does my grandmother shut down when I ask about her childhood?

When you ask broad questions like "What was your childhood like?", she may respond with a deflection such as, "It was just a normal time." This happens because you are asking her to summarize decades of experience into a single answer, which feels like a test or an evaluation. She may feel that her life was ordinary, or she may simply be tired of performing a version of herself that she thinks you want to hear.

The shutdown is a protective mechanism. It is not a rejection of you, but a reaction to the weight of the request. By asking for a narrative, you are asking her to curate, edit, and present her past, which is emotionally exhausting. The moment the conversation feels like a performance, the natural flow of memory stops.

## What is the "Sensory Anchor" technique for unlocking memories?

The "Sensory Anchor" technique involves asking about a specific physical detail rather than a general period of time. Instead of asking about her school, ask about the sound of the kettle whistling while she waited for it to boil. Instead of asking about her neighborhood, ask about the smell of the air after a bushfire.

Sensory details are harder to gatekeep because they are not subject to the same kind of self-editing as chronological facts. When you ask about a smell or a sound, you are inviting her back into a specific moment, and the rest of the story often follows naturally. Asking about the feel of a V8 Holden engine might open up more than asking about her first car.

## How do I ask about difficult times without triggering defensiveness?

Difficult times are often the most guarded parts of a life story. If you ask directly about a loss or a hardship, she may shut down or give a very brief, factual answer. The key is to approach these topics indirectly, through the lens of daily life.

Ask about what she cooked for Sunday dinner in 1965, rather than what happened that year. Ask about the food on the table, the clothes she wore, or the music on the radio. These details provide a context for the difficult events without forcing her to confront them head-on. If she chooses to bring up the hard parts, let her do so. If she does not, respect that boundary.

## What is the "Third-Person Bridge" for opening up closed-off topics?

The "Third-Person Bridge" is a technique where you ask about someone else to open up a topic that is personally sensitive. For example, if you want to talk about her marriage but she seems unhappy talking about it, ask, "What was your mother-in-law like at Christmas?"

This creates a safe distance. She can talk about the dynamics, the atmosphere, and the shared experiences without feeling like she is being judged on her own choices. It allows her to share the emotional truth of the time period while maintaining a layer of protection. This is a powerful way to navigate topics that feel too heavy to address directly.

## How do I handle the silence when she says "I don't want to talk about it"?

Silence is a signal that carries emotional weight, and when she says she does not want to talk about it, the best response is to accept it without pushing. You can say, "You don't have to finish that thought. We can just sit here." This validates her autonomy and shows that you are not there to extract information, but to be with her.

## What people get wrong

**Recording without permission.** Putting a phone on the table can make the conversation feel like an interrogation. The physical act of removing the recording device reduces the feeling of being documented, which is a primary cause of defensiveness. If you are struggling with this, see [how to record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>).

**Asking for a timeline.** People do not remember their lives in chronological order. They remember them in fragments, flashes, and feelings. Asking for a timeline forces them to organise their memories in a way that feels artificial and tiring.

**Correcting their memories.** If a date is wrong or a detail is off, let it go. You are not writing a history book; you are capturing how she remembers it. Arguing over facts kills the emotional flow of the conversation.

**Waiting for a "good time".** The good time does not come; health changes, and memory changes. A rough, casual conversation today is better than a perfect, formal interview that never happens.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get my nan to talk about her childhood?

Use sensory anchors and low-pressure techniques to help your grandmother open up, avoiding the defensive walls that block genuine family storytelling. Ask about specific smells, sounds, or textures from a particular day rather than broad chronological periods. This approach bypasses the defensive "I don't know" response because sensory memories are harder to gatekeep than factual history.

### Is it rude to record my grandmother if she doesn't know I'm doing it?

No, it is generally considered respectful to ask for permission first. If she is reluctant, do not record. The relationship is more important than the data, and a secret recording can damage the trust you are trying to build.

### What if she only talks about the same three stories over and over?

This is a sign of cognitive anchoring or a need for reassurance. Do not correct her; validate the emotion behind the story. Consult Dementia Australia guidelines for specific cognitive decline advice if you are concerned about her memory.

### Do I need a professional to help me get her to talk?

Not necessarily. Many families succeed with patience and the right questions. A professional can help if the conversation is consistently blocked or if you want to preserve the narrative in a book format, but the most important thing is the connection you build.

### What if she says "I have nothing interesting to say"?

This is a common deflection. Respond with, "I don't need it to be interesting, I just want to know what it felt like to be you." This shifts the focus from performance to personal experience, which is often easier for her to share.
