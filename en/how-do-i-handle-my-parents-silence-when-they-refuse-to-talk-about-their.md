# How do I handle my parent's silence when they refuse to talk about their past?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/a3cabed765f8beb922b7.delivery-v1.webp>)

Stop asking direct questions about the past. Instead, use "ambient listening" and indirect prompts—such as discussing old photographs, shared family rituals, or your own current challenges—to create a safe space where your parent feels invited to share, rather than interrogated. Respect their silence as a boundary, not a rejection.

You sit down with a Lipton tea in a chipped mug at 4pm on a Tuesday, you ask the question you’ve been holding onto for years, and the room goes quiet. It isn’t a hostile silence, exactly, but it is a wall. They change the subject, they offer a one-word answer, or they simply look at you with a polite, closed expression.

It is easy to feel rejected in that moment, as if your curiosity is an intrusion or your love is not enough to make them open up. But for many Australian parents, silence is not a punishment. It is a habit, a form of protection, and a cultural norm that says personal pain is private.

You are not failing them. You are just using the wrong tool for the job. Direct questions often trigger defensiveness, but indirect approaches can lower the barrier without them even realising it.

## Why does my Australian parent shut down when I ask about their childhood?

Australian culture often relies on the phrase "no worries" as a deflection mechanism that minimizes distress and avoids deep emotional engagement. From the post-war era through to the modern workplace, there is an unspoken expectation that you handle your problems without complaining or seeking sympathy. For many of our parents, sharing trauma or deep sadness was seen as a weakness, or something that would burden the family.

When you ask, "Tell me about your childhood," you are asking them to break a rule they have lived by for decades. It feels like an exposure. If you are worried that the process feels like an interrogation, you might find it helpful to read [how to record your dad's story without it feeling like an interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>), which covers how to keep the tone light and collaborative.

Their silence is often a way of keeping the peace. They are not hiding from you; they are hiding from the memory itself, and they are used to doing it alone.

## What is the difference between hearing and listening in this context?

Hearing is a passive reception of sound, such as nodding when a parent says, "It was a hard time," whereas active listening in this context requires you to attend to the unspoken boundaries and the emotional weight behind the brevity of their response.

When a parent shuts down, they are communicating a boundary. They are saying, "I am not ready for this depth right now." If you push past that boundary, you turn a safe conversation into a performance. True listening involves accepting their silence as a complete answer for that moment. It means letting the quiet sit in the room without trying to fill it with more questions.

This is a skill that takes practice. It is about trusting that the story will still be there tomorrow, or next week, when the pressure is off.

## How can I use "indirect prompts" to bypass defensiveness?

Instead of asking about the event, ask about the environment. This is where the "indirect prompt" comes in. It bypasses the cognitive defensiveness that comes with direct questions about feelings or trauma.

**The Photo Prompt.** Hold an old photograph and ask, "Who is that?" or "What was the house like?" rather than "How did you feel when you lived there?" The photo is a neutral object; it invites description, not confession.

**The Parallel Story.** Share a brief, low-stakes story of your own struggle, such as a stressful week at work or a minor frustration. This invites them to share a parallel experience without you having to ask for it. It levels the playing field.

**The Sensory Question.** Ask about smells, sounds, or tastes from a specific era. "What did the kitchen smell like on Sundays?" or "What was on the radio?" These questions are safe because they are about the world, not the self.

If they still give short answers, you can look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) for more ways to gently expand the dialogue without pressure.

## What should I do if my parent says "there’s nothing to tell"?

Take them at their word, at least for the moment. For many people, their life feels ordinary because they were there for all of it. They don't see the drama or the significance that we, as outsiders, might perceive.

You can gently challenge this by saying, "I know it might not seem like much to you, but I want to know what it was like for you." This shifts the focus from the importance of the event to the value of their perspective.

If they remain firm, let it go; do not argue. You can try again in a few weeks, or you can shift to a completely different topic. The goal is to keep the door open, not to kick it down.

## How do I handle the silence without feeling rejected?

This is the hardest part, as it is natural to feel that their silence is a reflection of your worth or your relationship, but it usually isn't.

Try to reframe the silence. Instead of thinking, "They don't want to talk to me," try thinking, "They are protecting themselves from a painful memory." This is a small shift, but it can make a big difference in how you feel during the conversation.

Remember that you are not their therapist; you are their child. Your role is to be a safe space, not a fixer. If they are not ready to share, your job is to be present, not to extract.

## When is it appropriate to stop trying to get them to talk?

If the conversation consistently ends in distress, or if your parent becomes visibly upset or withdrawn, it is time to stop. You are not failing by stopping; you are respecting their limits.

There is also a "stranger effect" at play here. Sometimes, people open up more easily to neutral third parties because they lack the family's emotional baggage. If you find that you are hitting a wall repeatedly, it might be worth considering professional facilitation.

A neutral third-party interviewer can help draw out stories that might otherwise remain silent, without the pressure of a family interview.

## What people get wrong

**Treating silence as a problem to be solved.** Silence is often a feature, not a bug; it is a way of managing pain. Trying to "fix" the silence by asking more questions usually makes it worse.

**Pushing for the "big" stories first.** The war, the loss, the trauma are the stories we want most, but they are the hardest to access. Start with the small, safe stories. The big ones often follow once trust is built.

**Taking it personally.** Their silence is about their past, not your present. It is not a rejection of you; it is a protection of their self.

**Waiting for the perfect moment.** There is no perfect moment; there is only the moment you have. A rough conversation today is better than a perfect one that never happens.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for my dad to change the subject when I ask about the war?

Yes, this is a common deflection mechanism, which is a psychological strategy used to avoid confronting painful or traumatic memories by redirecting the conversation. Try shifting to a sensory detail, such as asking what the base looked like or what they ate, rather than asking about the events themselves. This allows them to share the context without having to confront the emotional weight of the experience directly.

### Should I force the conversation if I think they have something to hide?

No, forcing creates defensiveness and can damage the trust you have built. Respect the silence and return to the topic later in a different context, perhaps using an indirect prompt like a photograph or a shared memory. If they are not ready, the story will still be there when they are.

### What if my parent says "you're too young to understand"?

This is often a fear of judgment or a belief that their pain is not relevant to your generation. Respond with curiosity about their perspective at the time, asking what they thought would happen or how they coped, rather than trying to validate your own understanding. This shows you value their experience, not just the outcome.

### Can I record the conversation if they are reluctant?

Only with explicit permission. If they are reluctant to talk, they will likely be reluctant to be recorded, which adds pressure. It is better to have a live conversation where you can take notes or simply listen, and then ask if they would mind you recording a specific, shorter segment later if they feel comfortable.

### Does silence mean they don't love me?

No, silence in this context is usually about their own past pain, not their current relationship with you. It is a way of managing their internal experience, not a reflection of their feelings for you. Your presence and patience are what matter most.
