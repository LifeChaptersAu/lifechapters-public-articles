# What Should I Ask My Grandparents About Their Migration To Australia?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/92289b39dd5ddb9143cd.delivery-v1.webp?hero=e6146a6d95e0187b>)

Ask about one moment rather than the whole journey: what made them decide to come, what the trip itself was like, and where they first lived in Australia. Start with a concrete detail — a smell, a meal, a room — and let their answer suggest your next question.

You are sitting across from someone who left one country and built a life in another, and the story of how they got here is often the one they tell least. It sits underneath a lot of what you already know about them — what they value, what they hold on to, why a particular song or smell can stop them mid-sentence.

Most of the time it goes untold simply because nobody asks. They assume you know the outline already, or that it was all a long time ago and hardly matters now, so the details stay where they are.

You do not need to be a historian to ask about it, and you are not gathering material for an archive. You are asking one person what it was actually like, which is a much easier conversation to start.

## Why should I ask my grandparents about their migration to Australia?

Because the record keeps the outline and almost none of the experience. Passenger lists and arrival papers hold names, ages and occupations; they hold nothing about the last night at home, who came to say goodbye, or what they thought on the first morning here.

Their own perspective is the part you cannot read anywhere else — the friends they left, what they could hear at night on the way over, the moment this stopped feeling like somewhere they were staying and started feeling like home. Hearing that from them, in their words, is a different thing from knowing the general history of the period.

Asking is also its own kind of respect. It may well be the first time anyone has invited them to tell it properly, at their own pace and without interruption, and the invitation often matters as much as the answers do.

## What are the 5 most important questions to ask about the journey?

Five questions carry most of the story, and they tend to work best in this order.

**Start with the decision.** What made them leave, and whose decision was it — theirs, a parent's, a partner's? What did they picture Australia would be like before they ever saw it?

**Then the journey itself.** Ask how they travelled, by sea or by air, and how long it took. What did they eat, what could they see, did they sleep, and who were they travelling with?

**Then the arrival.** What is the first thing they remember seeing when they got here? Was anyone waiting for them, and what was the first thing somebody said to them?

**Then the first home.** Where did they sleep that first night, and what was the place like? Who else was there, how long did they stay, and what did they sort out first — work, school, or finding people who spoke their language?

**Then what they made of it.** What surprised them most about the place, what did they miss, and what do they hope you remember about how it all went?

## How do I ask about the difficult parts without causing pain?

Follow their lead, and let the hard parts be optional. Not every migration story is a story of hardship, and it is not your job to go looking for one; if it is there, it will surface on its own once they are comfortable.

If they do go somewhere painful, don't rush to fill the silence or fix it. Let them sit with it as long as they want, and if they change the subject, go with them — you can come back another day, or leave it alone entirely.

If they mention being treated as an outsider, that is worth asking about gently, because it is their experience and it belongs in their story. Ask how they handled it and who helped, rather than how bad it was.

If you are struggling to break the ice, or they are hesitant to start at all, [how do you get a parent to talk about their life?](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-to-get-a-parent-to-talk-about-their-life>) covers the broader approach. The principles are the same: start small, be specific, and let them lead.

## What specific Australian details should I look for in their story?

Ask about the ordinary texture of arriving, because that is where the story actually lives.

Ask about the words. Which English words did they learn first, which ones did people keep having to repeat for them, and was there slang nobody had warned them about?

Ask about the food. What did they miss, what did they cook to feel closer to home, and what was the first Australian thing they tried and quietly decided against?

Ask about the weather and the work. Did they know what the heat would be like, and how did they manage that first summer? What was the first job, who gave it to them, and what did it pay?

Details like these anchor a story in a place and a year, and they keep your grandparents as people who felt the sun on the back of their necks and read the bus timetable wrong, rather than figures in a general history. ## What people get wrong

**Asking for a summary.** "Tell me about coming out here" is too big to answer, and it invites the potted version they have given a hundred times; ask about one moment instead, and the rest tends to follow.

**Correcting the facts.** If a date or a name comes out wrong, let it go — you are not writing a history book, you are keeping their account of it, and the moment you start fact-checking, the talking stops.

**Ignoring the language.** If they express themselves more easily in their first language, ask them to use it, because the feeling usually stays in the language it happened in. You can have it translated later, but you cannot re-record the warmth.

**Waiting for the right time.** There is rarely an obvious one, so take the ordinary opening — a family gathering, a birthday, or a quiet afternoon when they already seem in the mood to talk.

## How do I record this so it lasts for generations?

Ask first. "Would you mind if I recorded this, so the family can hear it in your voice?" Almost everyone says yes, and people speak more freely once they have agreed than they do when they find out afterwards.

Your phone is the easiest tool you have, so sit somewhere quiet with the telly off and the phone on the table between you, close enough to pick up a soft voice. It doesn't need to sound like a studio, but it does need to be listenable in ten years, and a quiet room does most of that work for you.

Afterwards, get the file off the phone the same week and keep a copy somewhere that is backed up, then name it properly: "Grandad — coming to Australia, 2026" will mean something to someone in thirty years, while "Audio 001" will not. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What if my grandparent came to Australia by plane rather than by boat?

Ask the same questions about the flight instead. What they carried, how long they were in the air, who they sat next to, what the airport looked like at the other end, and what the air felt like when the doors opened. The details are different, but the story is the same one.

### What if my grandparent doesn't want to be recorded?

Respect that, and take notes instead. Write down their exact phrases as they say them rather than tidying them up, then fill in the rest from memory as soon as you get home. Some people relax about it once they have told a story or two, so there is no harm in asking again another day.

### How long should a conversation about their migration be?

Twenty to forty minutes is usually plenty, and it is better to stop while they are still enjoying it than to push on until everyone is tired. Several short conversations tend to produce more detail than one long one, and they give your grandparent time to remember things in between.

### What if my two grandparents remember the same journey differently?

Keep both versions and don't try to settle it. Two people standing in the same place notice different things, and the disagreement is often the interesting part, because it shows what each of them was worried about or chose to keep. If the debating takes over, talk to them separately.

### Are old documents or photos from the trip worth asking about?

Yes, and they are some of the best prompts you have. A ticket, a letter, a passport photo or a luggage tag puts something in their hands and gives them a specific thing to talk about, which is far easier than answering from a standing start. Ask who took the photo, or who wrote the letter.

### Should my children be in the room while we talk?

It depends on your grandparent. Some open up more with great-grandchildren there, because they are talking to an audience who has never heard any of it, while others go quiet or keep things light. If you are unsure, have one conversation on your own first and invite the kids to the next one.
