# What To Do If Mum Says Her Life Isn't Interesting Enough To Write Down?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/af8d5b4b8b6896f3697c.delivery-v1.webp?hero=1020eb400e1e25d8>)

Reframe her dismissal as a lack of permission, not a lack of content. Stop asking for her "life story" and start asking for small, specific memories or objects. By removing the pressure of performance and validating her daily experiences as worthy, you bypass her internal critic and allow her to share what she already knows, without needing to feel like a public figure.

You have sat down with your mum, probably over tea, and you have asked the question that is stuck in your throat. You want her story. You want to know who she was before she was your mother, before the house, before the years of quiet routine. And she has looked at you, maybe with a small, apologetic smile, and said that her life is boring. That there is nothing interesting enough to write down.

It is easy to feel rejected in that moment, and you might think she does not value her own history or that she does not want you to know her, but that is rarely the case. For many women, especially those who grew up in a time when modesty was a virtue, the idea of being the subject of a story feels uncomfortable; it feels like showing off, and sometimes it just feels wrong.

This is not a content problem, because there is plenty of content. It is a permission problem. She has not given herself permission to take up space in a narrative, so your job is not to convince her that she is interesting, but to help her feel safe enough to let the details out.

## Why does mum say her life is boring?

For many Australian women of the 1950s and 60s, self-promotion was culturally discouraged. The role of the wife and mother was considered the primary work, and it was often viewed as private rather than public. When a man says he has an interesting life, he might be talking about a career, a war, or a business, but when a woman says the same, she is often talking about the quiet, invisible labour of holding a family together.

She has likely been told, directly or indirectly, that her daily life is ordinary. So when you ask for her story, her first instinct is to defend herself against the accusation that she is boring. She is not saying she has nothing to say; she is saying she does not think what she has to say matters.

This is a common barrier. ## How do I stop feeling rejected when she dismisses her own history?

Her "boring" comment is a defense mechanism, not a fact. It is a way of protecting herself from the vulnerability of being seen. If you take it as a personal rejection, it will shut you down, but if you take it as a signal that she is uncomfortable, you can adjust your approach.

Try to separate the person from the performance. She is not performing for an audience; she is just a person who has lived a life. When she says she is boring, you do not need to argue. You can simply say, "I don't think that's true, but I'd love to hear about one specific thing." This removes the pressure of a grand narrative and replaces it with a small, manageable request.

## What are low-pressure prompts to start a conversation?

Avoid questions that require a summary, because "tell me about your childhood" is too big and asks for a whole book. Instead, use the "Object Anchor" technique. Ask about a specific thing, a smell, a sound, or a place. This bypasses the need for a narrative arc and lets her access a memory directly.

Here are three questions that work well because they are specific and sensory, rather than evaluative:

- What was the first song you danced to?
- What did you smell when you woke up in your first home?
- What was your favourite toy, and what did it look like?

These questions are low-stakes and easy to answer. If she gives a one-word answer, do not push. You can look at [how to keep the conversation going if they give short answers](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) for ways to gently follow up without making it feel like an interrogation.

## How do I reframe "boring" as "unrecorded"?

The word "boring" implies a judgment of value, while the word "unrecorded" implies a state of being. It is neutral. It suggests that the story exists, but it is just sitting there, waiting to be captured.

When she says her life is boring, you can reframe it as a lack of documentation, not a lack of interest. "I know it feels like a lot of the same things, but I think the way you did them is interesting to me." This validates her experience without asking her to agree that it is historically significant. It keeps the focus on your curiosity, not her worth.

## What to do if she still refuses to talk?

If she continues to say no, or if she gives you very short, closed answers, respect that. You are not trying to win a debate; you are trying to open a door. If the door is closed, you can leave it as is.

Sometimes, the best thing you can do is simply listen. You do not need to record; you do not need to write. You can just sit with her and ask one question. If she answers, great; if she doesn't, that is also an answer. It might be helpful to look at [what to do if my grandparent refuses to record their life story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-do-i-do-if-my-grandparent-refuses-to-record-their-life-story>) for broader perspectives on respecting boundaries.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it as a content deficit.** Assuming that if she is boring, there is nothing to write. This is rarely true; it is usually a permission issue.

**Pushing for the "big" stories.** Asking about the war, the marriage, the loss; these are heavy; start with the light, the sensory, the domestic. The big stories often follow once she feels safe.

**Correcting her.** If a date is wrong or a detail is off, let it go. You are not writing a history book; you are capturing her memory. Accuracy is less important than the feeling of the moment.

**Making it a project.** If you bring a notebook, a recorder, and a list of questions, it becomes an interview, so keep it casual and in the moment.

## How do I know if she is ready to share more?

Watch for the shift in her body language. If she starts to lean in, if she offers a detail you didn't ask for, if she laughs or gets quiet in a thoughtful way, she is ready. If she keeps checking the clock, or if her answers are short and flat, she is not.

Trust her cues; if she wants to stop, let her stop; the goal is not to extract a biography. The goal is to create a space where she feels safe enough to share a piece of herself. That is enough.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What to do if mum says her life isn't interesting enough to write down?

Reframe her dismissal as a lack of permission, not a lack of content. Stop asking for her "life story" and start asking for small, specific memories or objects. By removing the pressure of performance and validating her daily experiences as worthy, you bypass her internal critic and allow her to share what she already knows, without needing to feel like a public figure.

### Is it okay to record her without her knowing?

No, consent is essential for trust and ethical preservation. Covert recording can damage the relationship if discovered, and it undermines the spirit of the conversation. Always ask first, and if she says no, respect that boundary.

### What if she gets emotional and cries?

Pause, validate her feelings, and let her lead the pace. Do not rush to fix it or move on. Sometimes, the emotion is the story. Give her time to settle, and then gently ask if she would like to continue or if she needs a break.

### How long should these conversations be?

End the conversation while she is still engaged or before she shows signs of fatigue. A brief, positive interaction is better than a long, tiring one. You can always have another conversation later.

### Can I ask her about difficult topics like divorce or loss?

Only if she brings them up. Forcing difficult topics can trigger trauma or reinforce her belief that her life is "too messy" to share. Let her set the pace, and be prepared to move to lighter topics if she becomes distressed.

### What if she says "I have nothing to say" after a good conversation?

Acknowledge her effort, thank her, and leave the door open. Do not push for more in the same session. You can say, "Thank you, that was really nice," and then change the subject. This leaves a positive impression and makes her more likely to agree to talk again in the future.
