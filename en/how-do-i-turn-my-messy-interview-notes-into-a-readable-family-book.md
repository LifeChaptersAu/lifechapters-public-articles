# How Do I Turn Interview Notes Into A Family Book?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/1b6af1ee60e680026cbe.delivery-v1.webp?hero=d506c3eca64f1ea5>)

Sort your notes into chronological buckets by life stage, such as childhood or career, before you start editing. Create a simple timeline to map where each story fits, then group related themes into chapters. This structure creates a natural flow, allowing you to write in your parent's voice without needing professional writing skills.

You have a shoebox of handwritten notes from a 1970s interview, or a hard drive of unlabelled voice memos, and you are staring at it. It feels like a mess. You know there is a story in there, but it looks like a pile of loose papers, and the idea of turning that into a book feels impossible.

This is not a failure of your writing skills, but rather a structural problem that arises when you try to edit before you have organised the material.

You do not need to be a writer. You need a way to sort the chaos so the story reveals itself.

## Why do my interview notes feel like a jumbled mess rather than a story?

Because they are raw material, not a narrative. When you interview a parent, you get anecdotes, fragments, and memories that jump around in time. One minute they are talking about their wedding, the next about a job in the 1970s.

This is normal, because human memory is not linear; it is associative. If you try to read these notes in the order you wrote them, they will feel disjointed. The mess is not a sign that you did a bad job; it is the natural state of unprocessed memory.

The goal is not to fix the notes, but to find the thread that connects them.

## What is the first step to organising raw interview data?

Avoid the urge to edit, rewrite, or worry about grammar and flow until you have first established a basic structure for the material.

Start with triage; this is a simple sorting process. Take your notes, whether they are on paper or in a document, and sort them into broad life stages. You can use sticky notes, or just create sections in a document.

Try these buckets:

- **Childhood and early years:** Where they grew up, school, first jobs.
- **Career and work:** Their professional life, the move from the farm to the city, the housing boom.
- **Family and relationships:** Marriage, children, friends, and major life events.
- **Later years:** Retirement, hobbies, and recent memories.

You do not need to be precise. If a story fits in two buckets, put it in the one that feels right. This step turns a wall of text into manageable piles.

## How do I group scattered anecdotes into coherent chapters?

Once you have your buckets, look for themes within them. A chapter is not just a time period; it is a theme.

For example, in the "Career" bucket, you might find several stories about their first job. Group those together; that is a chapter. In the "Family" bucket, you might find stories about their wedding and their first home. Group those.

A simple spreadsheet helps here. Create three columns:

1. **Date/Year:** When did this happen?
2. **Story:** A brief summary of the anecdote.
3. **Emotion/Theme:** What is the feeling or the point of this story?

Sort by date, then by theme; you will start to see patterns. Maybe there is a recurring theme of resilience, or a pattern of moving to new places. These patterns become your chapter titles.

If you want a deeper look at how to weave these threads together, see [how can I turn my parent's scattered stories into a coherent narrative](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-can-i-turn-my-parents-scattered-stories-into-a-coherent-narrative>).

## What is the best way to preserve my parent’s unique voice while editing?

This is the most important part. The soul of the book is not the facts; it is the way your parent speaks.

Keep their idioms. If they say "no worries, mate," do not change it to "no problem." If they use a specific phrase for a common thing, keep it. These details are what make the book feel like them, not like a biography written by a stranger.

When you edit, focus on clarity, not correctness. Remove the "ums" and "ahs," but keep the rhythm. If they repeat a word for emphasis, keep it. If they use a colloquialism that is specific to their region or generation, keep it.

The balance between emotional truth and factual precision is key here. You do not need to perfect every detail. The emotional truth of the story carries the most weight. The exact date of a minor event matters less than the feeling they had when it happened.

## How do I handle gaps or missing dates in the timeline?

You will have gaps and you will not know the exact year of every event, but that is okay.

Use approximations; "around 1965" is better than leaving it blank; if you are not sure, ask your parent. If they are not sure either, use "early 1960s" or "before the war."

Do not let the lack of a precise date stop you. The story is more important than the calendar. If a story is important, include it, even if the date is fuzzy.

## What people get wrong

**Editing as they go.** This is the biggest mistake, as people try to write the perfect sentence while they are still sorting the notes, which slows you down and makes you anxious; sort first; write later.

**Trying to include everything.** You do not need every story; some anecdotes are just filler. If a story does not reveal something about your parent's character or values, it is okay to leave it out.

**Ignoring the repetition.** If your parent repeats the same story, it is because it matters to them. Do not delete it just because it is repetitive. Instead, look at why they keep telling it; that is the theme. See [how do I handle it when my grandparent repeats the same story over and over](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-handle-it-when-my-grandparent-repeats-the-same-story-over-and>) for more on this.

**Waiting for perfection.** A rough draft is better than a perfect plan. Get the structure down, and you can refine it later.

## When should I stop trying to edit it myself?

If you are stuck, or if the task feels too heavy, it is okay to ask for help. You have done the hard work of collecting the stories. Structuring them is a different skill.

If you are [struggling to keep the conversation going](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>), or if you want to ensure the final piece reads with clarity and flow, you can reach out to a professional editorial service. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to transcribe the audio before I can organise the notes?

No, you can organise the key points and anecdotes from your notes first. Transcription is a later step for polishing. You can use your handwritten notes or a rough summary to build the timeline and chapters. Once the structure is clear, you can go back to the audio to fill in the details and capture the exact voice.

### What if my parent’s stories don’t follow a logical timeline?

That is normal. Use the "Theme" column in your spreadsheet to group related stories, even if they happened in different years. You can arrange the chapters by theme rather than by strict chronology. This often creates a more engaging narrative, as it groups similar experiences together and highlights the patterns in your parent's life.

### How do I decide which stories to cut from the final draft?

Prioritise stories that reveal character or values over those that are merely factual. If an anecdote does not help the reader understand who your parent is, it is safe to remove it. This keeps the narrative focused and prevents the reader from feeling overwhelmed by minor details that do not contribute to the emotional core of the story.

### How long should each chapter be?

There is no fixed word count, as the length should serve the narrative flow rather than a rigid standard. Focus on exploring the theme in depth without overwhelming the reader. You can adjust the length based on the complexity of the stories, ensuring each section feels complete and balanced.

### How do I turn interview notes into a family book without photos?

You can proceed with text-only sections, as the story is the primary content and photos are enhancements rather than requirements. Focus on the details in the text to convey the emotion and context. You can always add photos later if you find them, but the narrative will still be complete and meaningful without them.
