# How do I record my dad's story without it feeling like an interview?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/dd4b744cb18ab635fc26.delivery-v1.webp>)

Stop treating it like an interview. Instead, use "ambient listening": record while you are both doing something else, like driving, fixing a lawnmower, or fishing. When his hands are busy and you are not staring at him, the pressure vanishes. He will share stories naturally, and you can capture his voice and memories without the awkwardness of a formal Q&amp;A.

You sit in the driveway, phone in hand, and look at him. He’s polishing the car, or maybe he’s just leaning on the fence. You want to ask about his life, but the words stick in your throat because it feels like you’re about to interrogate him. You worry that if you ask the wrong thing, he’ll shut down, or that you’ll look like a child doing homework.

Many men in their fifties and sixties feel this specific hesitation when they want to capture their father’s history. It isn’t about the technology, and it isn’t about what [he has to](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) say. It is about the shape of the interaction. A formal question demands a formal answer, and most of us find that performance exhausting.

You don’t need to conduct an interview to save his story. You just need to change the setting.

## Why does asking my dad direct questions feel so awkward?

For many Australian men, the word "interview" carries a weight that goes beyond journalism. It implies a test, or a performance, or a judgment. When you sit face-to-face and ask, "Tell me about your childhood," you are asking him to perform a summary of his life while you watch. That is a high-pressure request, and he will often respond with a short, safe answer to end the discomfort.

The awkwardness comes from the power dynamic of the gaze. When you look at him, he feels seen. When he feels seen, he starts to edit himself. He chooses the safe stories, the ones that are easy to tell, and he leaves out the messy, real parts that are actually the most valuable.

## What is "ambient listening" and how does it work?

Imagine you are both in the car, driving to the hardware store. You are not looking at each other; you are looking at the road. He mentions a job he did twenty years ago, and you ask a small follow-up question about the tools he used. He answers without pausing to compose a speech. This is ambient listening: recording while you are both doing something else.

When you are side-by-side, the performance aspect disappears. You are not an interviewer; you are a companion in a task. He can look out the window, or at the engine, or at the water. This gives him space to think and to speak without the pressure of your attention. It feels like a chat, which is exactly what it is.

## How do I start a conversation without it feeling like a deposition?

Start with a specific memory, not a broad question. Instead of asking, "What was it like growing up?" which is too big and feels like a test, try, "Dad, do you remember when we went to Coffs Harbour?" or "Do you remember that summer we spent at the creek?"

Specific anchors pull out specific stories, inviting a narrative rather than a summary. If you need more structure, you can look at [what questions to ask your dad about his childhood](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-questions-should-i-ask-my-dad-about-his-childhood>) to find prompts that feel natural rather than formal.

## What are the best shared activities for recording a father’s story?

Choose activities that are repetitive and low-stakes. Driving to the hardware store is ideal, fixing a lawnmower in the driveway works well, and fishing at the local creek is great. These are things you have done together before, so there is no need to perform.

The key is that the activity should occupy your hands, not your mind. If you are trying to fix a complex electrical fault, you won’t be listening. If you are just driving a familiar route, you can focus on the conversation.

## How do I handle the silence when he doesn’t answer?

Silence is not a failure; it is often where the best stories begin. If you ask a question and he doesn’t answer immediately, don’t jump in to fill the gap. Wait. He might be searching for the right word, or he might be deciding if he wants to tell you.

If the silence stretches too long, you can offer a small detail of your own to bridge the gap. "I always thought that was weird," or "I remember that day because it was so hot." This gives him a hook to hang his story on.

## What do I do with the recordings once I have them?

First, get them off your phone. Voice memos are fragile; a phone upgrade or a lost handset can wipe them out. Copy them to a cloud drive or an external hard drive the same week you make them.

## What people get wrong

**Waiting for the perfect moment.** The perfect moment is usually a myth, and waiting for it often means the recording never happens because health changes and memory fades. A rough recording made this month is better than a perfect one that never happens.

**Correcting every detail.** If he gets a date wrong, or if a story has drifted, let it go. You are capturing how he remembers it, and that has its own truth. Arguing about facts turns a chat into a debate.

**Doing it in one long sitting.** An hour is plenty; two is too many; tiredness flattens a story, and the energy drops. It is better to have several short, high-energy sessions than one long, draining one.

**Thinking you need a studio setup.** You don’t; a smartphone voice memo app is sufficient; the goal is clarity, not production value. If the technology is too complex, it becomes a barrier to the conversation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a professional microphone to record my dad’s voice?

No. A smartphone’s built-in voice memo app is more than enough. The goal is clarity, not studio-quality production. The technology should be invisible so you can focus on the moment and the connection between you.

### What if my dad says he doesn’t have anything interesting to say?

This is usually a sign of vulnerability, not disinterest. Reframe the mission: it’s not about him being impressive, it’s about creating a legacy for his grandchildren. Use specific story-starters rather than broad questions to help him find the moments he actually remembers.

### Is it okay to record a conversation without telling him first?

No; always get consent. Explain that it’s for the family, for his grandchildren, and that he can stop at any time. Transparency builds trust and makes him feel respected rather than surveilled.

### How long should each recording session be?

Keep it short; 15–30 minutes is ideal; you can always do another session later. Shorter sessions feel less like an interview and more like a chat, which keeps the energy high and the stories flowing.

### What if the recording quality is bad?

It’s better to have a bad recording than no recording. If the audio is clear enough to understand, it’s valuable. The content is what matters, and the technical details can be handled later if needed.

### Can I use this technique for my mother too?

Yes. The "ambient listening" and "shared activity" techniques work for any parent. The key is to remove the pressure of a formal interview and let the conversation flow naturally, regardless of who is speaking.
