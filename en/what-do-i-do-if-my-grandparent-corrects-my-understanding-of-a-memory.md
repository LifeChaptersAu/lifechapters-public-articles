# What Do I Do If My Grandparent Corrects My Understanding Of A Memory?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/16154da46c2627e5af26.delivery-v1.webp?hero=e6a8e547dfdc6259>)

You do not need to choose between your memory and theirs. Acknowledge the elder's perspective with empathy, then gently ask for the details that differ. For the family record, capture both versions as complementary facets of the truth, ensuring the final narrative honors their emotional reality while preserving the factual core.

You are sitting at the kitchen table, the tick of the grandfather clock in the hallway marking the seconds, while the scent of Earl Grey tea steams from the cup between you. You are listening to a story you have heard before, when your grandparent says something that contradicts what you know. They look at you with a steady, certain gaze, and for a moment, you feel your own reality wobble. It is a strange, quiet feeling, like being gaslit by someone you love, and it can leave you unsure of whether to correct them, agree with them, or just change the subject entirely.

Memory is not a video recording; it is a living thing that shifts and reshapes itself over time. The goal here is not to win a debate about the facts, but to capture the truth of their experience while keeping the peace in the room.

We will look at why these differences happen, how to respond in the moment, and a practical method for recording both versions so that the final story feels complete rather than contested.

## Why does my grandparent's memory differ from mine?

Human memory is reconstructive, not reproductive. Every time we recall an event, we are actually rebuilding it from fragments, emotions, and later knowledge. This means that two people who were in the same room can have two equally valid, yet different, memories of what happened.

For older Australians, this is often compounded by the passage of time and the nature of migration. Many families have oral histories that conflict with official records due to the post-war migration schemes of the 1950s, where families often arrived on ships like the *Orford* or the *Kungurara*. A grandparent might recall moving to Australia in 1952, while a birth certificate or a family photo shows 1954. They may insist on 1952 because that is when they *felt* they started their new life. Their memory is anchored to their emotional journey, not just the calendar.

Understanding this distinction is key. When they correct you, they are often not trying to be factually precise; they are trying to convey the significance of the event. Their version is the truth of their experience, even if the date is off.

## How do I respond when they correct a fact I remember clearly?

The instinct is to defend your own memory. You might think, "But the photo says 1954," or "The records show otherwise." While this is logical, it often shuts down the conversation. If you challenge the fact, they may feel their experience is being invalidated, and the story stops.

Instead, try a response that validates their perspective without necessarily agreeing on the data. You might say, "I remember it being 1954, but I can see why 1952 feels like the right time for you. What was it about that year that made it feel like the start?"

This approach does two things. It acknowledges their certainty, which lowers their defensiveness, and it pivots the conversation back to the story. You are no longer arguing about a date; you are exploring the feeling behind the date. This keeps the tap open for the narrative, which is what you are actually there to collect.

## What is the difference between a memory error and a deliberate change?

It is helpful to distinguish between a simple lapse in recall and a narrative that has evolved over time. A memory error is usually a small detail, like a name or a date, that slips. A deliberate change, or a "therapeutic" adjustment, is when a person reshapes a story to fit their current self-image or emotional state.

For example, a grandparent might change a job title from "factory worker" to "production manager" to sound more prestigious, or soften a harsh truth about a parent. This is not deception; it is a way of making sense of a life. When you notice this, it is not your job to fact-check them. Your job is to listen to the story they are telling you now, because that is the version they have chosen to live with.

If you suspect that the changes are due to cognitive decline rather than narrative evolution, it is worth considering a professional perspective. If dementia is suspected, refer to [Dementia Australia guidelines](<https://www.dementia.org.au/>) for person-centred communication, but do not diagnose. In the meantime, focus on the emotional connection rather than the clinical details.

## How do I record both versions without taking sides?

This is where the practical work begins. If you are taking notes or recording audio, you need a way to hold both truths without letting one erase the other. The "Two-Column Method" is a simple technique that works well for this.

Use a legal pad and draw a line down the centre of the page. On the left side, write down exactly what your grandparent says, in their words, including their dates and their details. On the right side, note the factual cross-reference: the dates from the birth certificate, the names from the family tree, the context you know to be true.

You do not merge them in the moment; instead, you let both columns exist. Later, when you are editing or writing the final piece, you can weave them together. You might write, "Grandpa remembers the move as happening in 1952, the year he felt he truly began his Australian life, though the official records show the family arrived in 1954." This honors his emotional truth while preserving the factual core. It is a way of saying, "Both of these things are true."

## When should I stop the conversation if it becomes distressing?

Sometimes, the correction is not just about the fact; it is a trigger. Mentioning a late spouse or a specific location like the old family home in Melbourne can cause sudden distress. In these moments, the goal shifts from collecting the story to protecting the relationship.

If you notice their distress rising, it is okay to pause. You might say, "I can see this is a lot to talk about. Let's take a break and come back to it later." This is not a failure; it is a sign that you are paying attention to their emotional state.

If the conflict becomes too heavy, or if the conversation turns into a repetitive argument, it may be time to step back. You can explore [what to do if your grandparent refuses to record their life story](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-do-i-do-if-my-grandparent-refuses-to-record-their-life-story>) for alternative ways to connect without the pressure of a formal interview. Sometimes, the best thing you can do is simply be present, without the agenda of recording.

## How do I validate their experience while preserving the truth?

Validation does not mean agreement. It means acknowledging the importance of the event to them. You can say, "I can see how much that meant to you," or "It sounds like that was a really significant time." This validates their emotional reality.

Then, you can gently preserve the truth by asking for the details that matter to them. "What was the first thing you did when you got there?" This keeps the focus on their experience, which is the heart of the legacy.

## What people get wrong

**Arguing for the sake of accuracy.** This is the most common mistake, as you are not writing a legal brief but preserving a life. Winning the argument about the date loses the story about the feeling.

**Assuming they are confused.** Many older adults have perfectly clear minds but memories that have shifted. Assuming cognitive decline can be dismissive and hurtful. Always give them the benefit of the doubt that their version is the one they have chosen to live with.

**Trying to fix it in the moment.** You do not need to resolve the discrepancy while you are sitting there. Let the two versions coexist. The editing is where the reconciliation happens, not in the kitchen.

**Ignoring your own feelings.** If you feel gaslit or invalidated, that is a real reaction. It is okay to step away, take a breath, and return to the conversation later with a calmer mind. Your emotional well-being matters too.

Ask permission before recording, let the person choose the pace, and pause if they become tired or distressed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do I do if my grandparent corrects my understanding of a memory?

Acknowledge their perspective with empathy and ask for the details that differ, rather than challenging the fact directly. This keeps the conversation open and allows you to record both their emotional truth and the factual context without invalidating their experience.

### How do I record both versions of a conflicting memory?

Use a two-column method to write down their version on one side and the factual details on the other. This allows you to preserve their emotional truth while keeping the accurate record, so you can weave them together later without taking sides.

### What if they are clearly confused and not just mistaken?

If confusion is frequent or severe, it is wise to consult a healthcare professional for a proper assessment. For legacy purposes, however, you can still focus on the emotional truth of their story rather than forensic accuracy.

### How do I handle it if my parents disagree with my grandparent's version?

Acknowledge the complexity of the situation. The family record can reflect that different family members have different perspectives on the same event, and that is a valid part of the family history.

### Is it normal for grandparents to change their stories over time?

Yes, memory is reconstructive, and people often update narratives to fit their current emotional state or self-image. This is a natural part of how humans process their past, and it does not necessarily indicate confusion or dishonesty.
