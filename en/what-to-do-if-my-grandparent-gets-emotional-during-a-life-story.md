# What to do if my grandparent gets emotional during a life story interview?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/4d0faec63e4486aff00e.delivery-v1.webp>)

Stop the recording immediately and do not ask them to keep going. Acknowledge the feeling by saying, "I can see this is a heavy memory," and let the silence sit for at least ten seconds. When they are ready, ask, "Would you like to talk about what that felt like, or shall we move to a lighter memory?" This validates their experience without forcing them to perform.

You are sitting at the kitchen table, the recorder is on, and then your grandparent starts to cry. You freeze, your hand hovering over the stop button, unsure whether to press it or hold your breath. Your first instinct is to rush in with tissues and apologies, to say *sorry, I didn't mean to upset you*, and to quietly stop the recording. You feel like you have failed, like you have hurt them, but this hesitation is a shared experience for many of us. It is not a failure; it is the moment the story becomes real.

Watching a parent or grandparent grieve a memory we cannot fully see is terrifying. We worry we are prying, or that we are causing pain, or that we are ruining the memory by trying to capture it. You are not ruining it; you are witnessing it. And the way you handle the next thirty seconds matters more than the questions you asked before.

## Why does my grandparent’s tears feel like I’ve failed the interview?

Because we are trained to think that a good interview is a smooth one. We imagine a steady flow of facts, dates, and anecdotes, like a well-edited documentary. When the voice cracks, when the hands shake, when the silence stretches out, it feels like a glitch, like the machine has broken.

But for your grandparent, the emotion is not a glitch. It is the point; the facts are just the skeleton. The tears, the pause, the breath, the way they say *I miss him*—that is the flesh and blood. If you smooth it out, if you rush to fix it, you lose the very thing that makes the story matter to the next generation.

This is especially true in Australian families, where there is a strong cultural tendency to tough it out. For many grandparents born in the 1930s or 1940s, showing emotion was often discouraged. They were taught to be stoic, [to keep a](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-keep-the-conversation-going-if-they-give-short-answers>) stiff upper lip, to minimise grief. When they finally let the tears come, it may feel like a loss of control to them, not just sadness. They may look at you with a mix of relief and shame, wondering if they have let you down.

## What is the "Pause-Validate-Resume" method for emotional moments?

This core technique is simple in its structure but requires you to resist your own anxiety by staying present with their distress rather than rushing to fix it.

**Pause.** Stop the recording immediately and do not keep it rolling while they are in distress. The act of stopping the machine signals that the conversation is no longer a task; it is now a human moment, so put the phone down and sit back.

**Validate.** Acknowledge the feeling without trying to fix it; say something simple and true, such as *I can see this is a heavy memory* or *That sounds like it was a really hard time*. Do not say *it’s okay* or *don’t worry*, because those phrases can feel dismissive; just name what is happening.

**Resume.** Wait and let the silence sit; when they are ready, offer them a choice, such as *Would you like to talk about what that felt like, or shall we move to a lighter memory?* This is the "Two-Option" question; it gives them agency. They are not being forced to continue, and they are not being forced to stop. They are choosing.

## How do I know if they want to continue or stop?

Watch their body, not just their words. If they are crying but still looking at you, still speaking, they want to continue. If they are looking away, shaking their head, or saying *I can’t*, they want to stop.

Trust their lead. If they say *I don’t want to talk about this*, respect it immediately. Say, *Okay, let’s move on.* Do not push; do not ask *are you sure?* You can revisit the topic later, or never; their boundary is the rule.

If the distress is severe or prolonged, it is okay to pause the project entirely. If you are worried about memory issues or deeper emotional health, consider speaking with their GP or a counselor. Resources like [Dementia Australia](<https://www.dementia.org.au/>) can offer guidance if you suspect cognitive changes are affecting the conversation.

## What should I say when the silence feels too long?

Nothing; that is the answer.

This is the hardest part; your brain will start to panic, and you will want to fill the silence with chatter, with *so, what about the house?*, with anything to make the air move; resist this.

Wait until the emotional wave visibly subsides before you offer a word. This allows the feeling to crest and settle naturally. If you jump in too early, you interrupt their processing. If you wait, you give them space to find their footing.

Have a glass of water and a tissue box within arm’s reach, not just on the table. The physical act of drinking water can help reset the nervous system. It gives them something to do with their hands, something to focus on other than the emotion.

## How do I handle it if they ask me to stop recording?

Stop immediately; do not ask them to explain why; do not say *but I just started*; just stop.

If they ask you to stop, it is because they feel unsafe, or overwhelmed, or simply not ready. Their comfort is more important than the data; you can always start again another day. You cannot repair the trust if you push them to continue against their will.

## What people get wrong

**Rushing to fix it.** The moment they cry, you start talking, asking if they are okay, if they want water, or if you should stop. This turns a moment of vulnerability into a logistical problem. Let the silence do the work.

**Treating it as a failure.** If you act [like the interview](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/how-do-i-record-my-dads-story-without-it-feeling-like-an-interview>) has gone wrong, they will feel like they have gone wrong. They will start to hide their emotions to avoid "ruining" the session.

**Ignoring the Australian context.** Assuming that everyone wants to talk about their feelings. For some, the tears are a breach of their own code. Acknowledge that, and they will feel understood.

**Forgetting the "Authentic Voice" argument.** Thinking that a "perfect" recording is the goal; it is not. The crack [in the voice,](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) the pause, the breath—these are the most human parts. A smooth interview is often the least memorable.

## Is it better to turn the camera off or keep it rolling?

If they are crying but still talking, keep it rolling. The emotion is part of the story; if they ask you to stop, stop. If they are in distress and not talking, stop.

The goal is not to capture every second; the goal is to capture the truth; and sometimes, the truth is a pause.

Ask permission before recording, let the person choose the pace, and pause if they become tired or distressed. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What if my grandparent gets angry instead of sad?

Anger is often a mask for grief or fear. Use the same pause-validate-resume method; acknowledge the intensity by saying, "I can hear how strong this memory is." Do not argue or correct them. Let the emotion be heard without trying to fix it or explain it away.

### Should I turn off the recording if they cry?

Only if they ask you to. If they are crying but still talking, the recording captures the emotion, which is valuable. If they ask you to stop, stop immediately; their comfort is more important than the data; you can always start again another day.

### How long should I wait before resuming?

There is no fixed time. Wait until they make eye contact or take a deep breath. It could be a few seconds or several minutes. Follow their lead, not your anxiety. The right time is when they are ready to speak again.

### What if they say "I don't want to talk about this"?

Respect it immediately; say, "Okay, let's move on." Do not push; you can revisit the topic later, or never; their boundary is the rule. If you push, you risk damaging the trust you have built.

### Is it normal to feel guilty for recording?

Yes. Many adult children feel they are "using" their grandparent. Remind yourself that you are preserving their voice for their family. The guilt is a sign you care, not that you are doing something wrong. It is a natural part of the process.

### Can I edit out the crying parts?

You can, but consider keeping them. The raw emotion is often the most valuable part for the family, as it conveys the depth of the memory in a way that words alone cannot. Removing it may make the story feel less authentic to the next generation.
