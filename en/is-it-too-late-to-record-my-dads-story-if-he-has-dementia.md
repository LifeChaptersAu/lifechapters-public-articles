# Is It Too Late To Record My Dad's Story If He Has Dementia?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/14eb51404db3e29eb379.delivery-v1.webp?hero=f397361a2b9b6c63>)

It is not a simple yes or no. Dementia changes *how* you record, not necessarily *whether* you can. You can often capture voice, emotion, and sensory memories. The goal shifts from a chronological biography to preserving the essence of who they are.

You are sitting in the kitchen, holding the chipped blue mug that has been yours since university, while the radio plays that one song they always hum along to. You are holding a phone with the record button glowing red, waiting for a story that isn't coming.

A heavy silence fills the room, and you are worried that you have missed the window. You are worried that the person sitting across from you is slipping away, and that your hesitation has cost you something permanent.

That fear is real, but it is also the main thing stopping you.

You are not a technician; you are a child trying to hold on to a parent. That is enough. You do not need to be perfect, and you do not need to have started years ago. You just need to be present now.

## Why the fear of "too late" is holding you back?

The idea that there is a deadline is a myth we create to protect ourselves. If it is "too late," we do not have to try. If we do not try, we cannot fail.

But this logic is wrong. Dementia does not erase a person all at once; it changes the texture of their memory and moves the furniture around. The facts may get fuzzy, but the feelings often remain, as does the voice and the way they look at you.

When you focus on getting the dates right, you miss the point, which is not a biography but a connection.

## What changes in the recording process at each stage of dementia?

You do not need a medical chart. You need to watch how they are responding today.

**Early stage:** They can still tell long stories, but they may get stuck on recent events. Distant memories are often sharper. Ask about their childhood, their first job, or the house they grew up in. These memories are anchored in strong sensory details. Stop when they start repeating themselves or show signs of fatigue. Do not push.

**Middle stage:** Narrative becomes harder, and they may struggle to link events in a sequence. Switch to sensory prompts; instead of "What did you do in 1960?", ask "What did your mother’s kitchen smell like?" Use photos as triggers. Hold up a picture and ask, "Who is this?" or "Where were we?" Focus on their engagement level; if their attention wanders or they become agitated, it is time to pause. Their attention span is shorter, and that is okay.

**Late stage:** Words may be few or absent; this is where the recording becomes most powerful; you are no longer capturing facts; you are capturing the sound of their life. Their tone, their breathing, the way they hum a tune, the way they react to your voice. This is the legacy; it is not a transcript; it is a presence.

## How to ask questions when long conversations are no longer possible?

When language gets difficult, simple questions work best. They do not need to be complex to be meaningful.

Here are three questions that work even when the person is confused:

- "What is your favourite song?"
- "Who was your best friend?"
- "What is your happiest memory?"

These questions are open enough to allow for a story, but simple enough to be answered with a name, a title, or a feeling. They do not require a timeline; they require a connection.

## What to do when they stop recognising you?

This is the hardest part; it is not a failure of your love; it is a symptom of the disease.

Do not correct them or say "I am your daughter." If they think you are a nurse, or a stranger, or a friend, let them be in that reality for a moment. They are safe in that reality.

Instead, focus on the interaction; sit with them; hold their hand. Talk to them about the weather, or the food, or the music playing in the room. Record the sound of their voice responding to you. Even if they do not know who you are, they know that someone is there. That is enough.

## Why a "perfect" story is less important than a present one?

A perfect story is a document; a present story is a relationship.

Your goal is not to create a historical record. Your goal is to create a moment of connection. If the story is messy, if the dates are wrong, if the sentences are fragmented, it is still real. It is still them.

## What people get wrong

**Waiting for a clear moment.** There is no clear moment; there is only now. The clarity you are waiting for may not arrive in the way you expect.

**Focusing on the facts.** Facts are fragile, while feelings are durable, so a wrong date is less important than a true emotion.

**Doing it alone.** You do not have to be the interviewer; you can be the listener. You can be the one who holds the phone while someone else asks the questions.

**Thinking it is a one-time event.** It is not; it is a series of small moments; a conversation at breakfast; a song in the afternoon; a look across the room; collect them all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I record if my parent is in a care facility?

Yes, you can usually record in a care facility, but you should check the specific rules of that home first. Many facilities allow family members to bring in personal recording devices, though some may have quiet hours or restrictions on electronic equipment that you need to respect.

### How long should each recording session be?

Keep sessions short, typically between five and fifteen minutes, to avoid overwhelming your parent. Dementia can cause rapid fatigue or agitation, so it is better to have several brief, positive interactions than one long, draining conversation that leaves them feeling stressed.

### What if my parent refuses to talk to me?

Respect their refusal and do not force the conversation, as this can increase their anxiety. You might try a different approach later, such as listening to music together or looking at photos without asking direct questions, which can sometimes lower their defenses and open up a natural connection.

### Do I need to edit the recordings before sharing them?

You do not need to edit the recordings to make them perfect, as the raw, unpolished nature of the conversation is often what makes it feel authentic. You can simply trim the beginning and end to remove any awkward silences, but leaving in the pauses and natural speech patterns preserves the true essence of their voice.

### Can I record if my parent is non-verbal?

Yes, you can still record meaningful moments even if your parent is non-verbal. Focus on capturing the sounds of their breathing, their reactions to music, or the tone of their sighs, as these non-verbal cues are a powerful part of their presence and can be deeply comforting for the family to hear later.

### Is it okay to record in a public place?

It is generally better to record in a private, familiar space like your home to ensure your parent feels safe and comfortable. Public places can be distracting and noisy, which may make it harder for your parent to focus on the conversation and could lead to them feeling exposed or anxious.
