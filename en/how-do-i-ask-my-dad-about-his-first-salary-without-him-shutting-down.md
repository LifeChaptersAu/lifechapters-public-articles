# How Do I Ask My Dad About His First Salary?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/16154da46c2627e5af26.delivery-v1.webp>)

Don't ask for the number; ask for the feeling. Instead of "How much did you earn?", try "What did you do with your first paycheck?" This shifts the conversation from a defensive financial audit to a shared memory of survival and pride, allowing him to share the story without feeling judged on the amount.

You are sitting across from your dad, the air thick with the smell of old oil and sawdust from the workshop he spent decades in, and you want to know about his first job. You want to know how he felt when he got that first pay slip, what he bought, who he was working for. But the moment the topic of money comes up, you feel the temperature drop. You worry that asking about his salary will feel like an audit, or worse, like you are interested in [what he has](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) rather than who he is.

This is a very common fear, especially with fathers. You may have noticed that when bills are discussed, his jaw tightens and he stops making eye contact, a physical reaction that signals the topic is sensitive. Asking about his first salary can feel like poking at that tension. You are not trying to pry; you are trying to connect. But the method matters.

If you have been holding back because you don't want to make things awkward, the goal is not to extract a figure from 1975 or 1985. The goal is to open a door to a memory that he likely holds close to his identity.

## Why does asking about money feel like an attack on my dad’s pride?

For many men, their work history is a core part of their self-worth. Consider a mechanic who spent years in an apprenticeship; his first full salary was not just income, but tangible proof that his hands and mind were competent enough to be trusted with expensive machinery. Money, in their minds, is tied to their competence and their sacrifice. When a child asks, "How much did you earn?", it can inadvertently sound like, "Was it enough?" or "Did you do well?".

This is not a conscious judgment on his part, but the framing of the question can trigger a defensive response. He might not want to discuss the amount because it feels like a performance review. By shifting the focus from the value of the wage to the experience of earning it, you remove the judgment. You are asking about his journey, not his balance sheet. This distinction is crucial for keeping the conversation warm and open.

## What are three low-pressure ways to open the conversation about his first job?

You do not need a formal script, but having a few gentle entry points can help. The key is to ask about the sensory details and the narrative, not the data.

**Ask about the first purchase.** "What was the first thing you bought with your own money?" This is a safe question because it is about achievement, not income. It might be a car, a suit, or a gift for his mother. Whatever it is, it is a concrete object that anchors the memory.

**Ask about the environment.** "Where did you work, and what was it like?" This invites him to describe the noise, the people, the routine. It is a story about his world, not his wallet.

**Ask about the feeling.** "Did you feel proud or scared when you got that first pay slip?" This asks for an emotion, which is harder to defend against than a number. It acknowledges that starting out is often a mix of excitement and anxiety, and it validates his experience.

If you find that the conversation is starting to feel like an interrogation, the tone you set is just as important as the words you use.

## How do I keep the story going if he gives a one-word answer?

Sometimes, no matter how gently you ask, the response is short. "It was fine." "I don't remember." "It was hard." When this happens, it is easy to feel rejected or to push for more, which usually makes it worse.

A one-word answer is often a pause, not a wall. He might be processing, or he might be unsure of how much to share. In these moments, it helps to have a follow-up that is specific and low-pressure. [If he says the work was hard](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/what-to-say-when-dad-answers-in-three-words>), you could say, "I imagine that must have been tough. What was the hardest part of the day?" This acknowledges the silence rather than demanding more information.

If the conversation stalls, do not force it; you can always return to it later. The aim is to keep the door open, not to kick it down.

## What should I do if he changes the subject or gets defensive?

If he changes the subject, let him. It is a signal that the current path is not comfortable for him right now. You can gracefully pivot to a related but safer topic, such as the first tool he broke on the job or a specific memory about a coworker.

If he becomes defensive, it is important to back off and reassure him that you are not judging. You might say, "I'm just curious about what it was like starting out. I don't need the numbers, I just want to hear the story." This clarifies your intent and removes the pressure.

If you find that he consistently shuts down when money is mentioned, it may be worth exploring other avenues. Navigating these boundaries with patience and respect is key.

## Why is the "first salary" story actually about his identity, not his income?

The first salary is rarely just about the money. It is a milestone. It is the moment he became an adult, or at least felt like one. It is the foundation for the life he built, including the family home and the education of his children.

When you ask about his first job, you are asking about his origin story. You are asking about the person he was before he was a father, before he was a provider. This story is powerful because it connects his past to your present. By framing the question around identity and growth, you show him that his life has meaning beyond its financial outcomes.

## What people get wrong

**Treating it as a finance question.** If you approach the conversation with a spreadsheet mentality, asking for dates and amounts, you will likely get a shut-down because the goal is a story, not a record.

**Pushing when he resists.** If he says he doesn't want to talk about it, respect that. Pressing harder will only reinforce the idea that you are prying. Give him space and try again another day, or choose a different topic.

**Correcting his memory.** If he says he started in 1978 but it was actually 1979, let it go. You are not checking facts; you are listening to his perspective. Arguing over details will kill the momentum.

**Expecting a monologue.** The conversation may be short and fragmented; that is okay. Even a few minutes of genuine sharing is a victory. Do not feel like you need to fill every silence or extract an hour of content.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it rude to ask my dad how much he earned in the 70s?

No, it is not rude if you frame it as curiosity about his life rather than an audit of his finances. He likely remembers the feeling of that income and what it represented for his family more than the specific number. Asking about the experience is a sign of respect for his history.

### What if my dad says he doesn't remember his first salary?

This is a common deflection, not necessarily a lack of memory. You can pivot to a related question like, "What was the first thing you bought with your own money?" or "Who was your first boss?" These questions often open the same memories without the pressure of discussing figures.

### Does asking about money make me seem like I want his inheritance?

It can, if asked bluntly or with a tone that suggests an interest in his assets. By focusing on the story of his first job and the feeling of earning that money, you signal that you are interested in his labor and his journey, not his wealth.

### How do I ask about his financial struggles without making him feel ashamed?

Use inclusive language or historical context. Instead of asking about his debt, you might say, "I imagine things were tight back then. What was the hardest part of making ends meet?" This acknowledges the difficulty without singling him out for judgment.

### Can I ask about his salary if he was a manual worker?

Yes, the emotional weight of the first wage is universal, whether it was for a trade apprenticeship or a professional role. For many manual workers, the first paycheck was a significant milestone of independence and pride, and the story is just as valuable as any other.
