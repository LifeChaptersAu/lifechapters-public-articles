# What Specific Questions Should I Ask My Dad About His Trade Apprenticeship?

![A LifeChapters family story book being shared with family.](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog-assets/hero/d5b55f1e51195a03f9d3.delivery-v1.webp?hero=a86e6974e3e189ce>)

Ask about the specific sensory details of his first day, the exact moment he felt competent, the name of the foreman who shaped his work ethic, and the one technical problem he solved that he is still proud of. These questions bypass generic praise and reveal the concrete skills, mentorship dynamics, and professional pride that define his trade identity.

You have sat down with your dad, maybe over a cup of tea or a cold drink, and you want to know what it was really like to learn his trade. You have tried asking, "How was your apprenticeship?" and he has shrugged, said something like "It was alright," and changed the subject. It is frustrating, because you know there is more there. You know he spent years learning to do something difficult, and you want to understand that part of who he is.

This is not about getting a resume, the dates he started, or the certificate he earned; it is about the feeling of the work, the people who taught him, and the moments when he realised he was good at it. The problems with generic questions are that they are too big and too abstract. A trade is the weight of a hammer in your hand, the specific grit of sandpaper on wood, and the exact temperature of a soldering iron. To get the real story, you need to ask about the physical reality of the job.

## Why do generic questions like "How was it?" fail to capture a trade story?

A trade is not a concept; it is a set of physical actions. When you ask "How was it?", you are asking for an emotional summary of a physical process. Most tradespeople, especially those from older generations, are not comfortable summarizing emotions. They are comfortable describing what they did.

If you ask, "What was the hardest part of the job?", he might say "Nothing really." But if you ask, "What was the heaviest thing you had to carry on your first day?", he might remember the specific bag of cement or the roll of copper pipe. The specific detail opens the door to the memory, whereas the generic question keeps it closed.

## How do I ask about his mentors and the people who shaped his work ethic?

In Australian trades, the mentor is often the foreman or the boss. This person was not just an employer; they were the source of authority, skill, and often discipline. Asking about them reveals the culture of the trade.

Ask, "Who was the person you learned the most from?" or "What was one thing your foreman taught you that you still use?" These questions frame the relationship as one of learning, which is a positive and safe way to approach it. You might find that [he has a](<https://lifechapters.com.au/blog/can-i-record-my-mothers-voice-if-she-has-early-stage-dementia>) lot to say about a specific person, and that story will tell you more about his values than any list of skills.

## What questions uncover the physical and sensory reality of the job?

Trades are physical, involving heat, cold, dust, noise, and weight, and these sensory details serve as the anchors of memory that are harder to forget than dates or facts. Ask about the smell of sawdust, the weight of a specific tool, or the sound of a specific machine. For example, if he was a carpenter, ask about the smell of the timber. If he was an electrician, ask about the heat of the soldering iron. These questions bypass the intellectual part of the brain and go straight to the physical memory.

## How do I handle it when he minimizes his own achievements?

Many tradespeople are humble. They might say, "It was just a job," or "I didn't do anything special." This is a common response, and it does not mean the story is not there. It means he is not used to being asked for it.

You are not asking for praise; you are asking for the process, and the process is where the pride lives.

## What people get wrong

**Asking for a chronological timeline.** This is the most common mistake, as a timeline is a list of facts rather than a story. It is better to ask about specific moments and let the timeline emerge naturally from the details.

**Asking "Did you enjoy it?"** This is too broad; it invites a yes or no answer. Instead, ask about the specific parts of the day he liked or disliked.

**Assuming he remembers dates.** Do not ask, "When did you start?" Ask, "What was the first job you did?" The date will come out in the details, or it will not matter.

**Correcting his memory.** If he says he started in 1975 and it was actually 1976, do not correct him. You are not writing a history book; you are capturing his memory; his memory is the truth for this story.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What specific questions should I ask my dad about his trade apprenticeship?

Start with sensory details like the smell of the workshop or the weight of his first tool, as these physical cues often trigger memories better than dates or abstract concepts. You can also ask about the specific foreman who taught him the most or the one technical problem he solved that he is still proud of, which bypasses generic praise and reveals the concrete skills and mentorship dynamics that define his trade identity.

### How do I ask about his boss without it sounding negative?

Frame the question around learning and skill acquisition rather than authority or conflict. Ask, "Who was the person you learned the most from?" or "What was one thing your foreman taught you that you still use?" This approach focuses on the positive aspects of the relationship and the skills he gained, rather than any potential conflicts.

### Is it too late to ask these questions if he is in his 80s?

No. Even if his memory is fading, the emotional resonance of the trade remains. Focus on the feeling of competence and pride rather than technical specifications. The story is about who he was, not just what he did.

### What if he was in a trade that is now obsolete?

Ask about the distinct skills that are no longer taught. What did he know that modern tradespeople do not? This highlights his expertise and the evolution of the industry. It is a chance to appreciate the specific knowledge he carried with him.

### Should I record the conversation?

Yes, if he is comfortable. A recording captures the tone, the pauses, and the specific vocabulary he used, which is crucial for preserving the "voice" of the trade. It allows you to hear the nuances that are lost in written notes.
